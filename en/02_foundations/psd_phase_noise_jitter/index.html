Phase Noise → Jitter

Prerequisites: white_noise_to_phase_noise · stochastic_noise_basics | Next: serdes_clocking_connection

This page answers the question engineers ask most often: you are handed a phase-noise plot (L(f)\mathcal{L}(f)L(f), dBc/Hz) — how do you turn it into a single time-domain number, the rms jitter σt\sigma_tσt​ (fs)? This is the bridge between the frequency domain (the language of communications/RF) and the time domain (the language of digital/SerDes).

The full chain is four steps:

L(f) → ×2, de-dB  Sϕ(f) → ∫f1f2  σϕ2 →    σϕ → ÷(2πf0)  σt.\mathcal{L}(f)\ \xrightarrow{\ \times2,\ \text{de-dB}\ }\ S_\phi(f)\ \xrightarrow{\ \int_{f_1}^{f_2}\ }\ \sigma_\phi^2\ \xrightarrow{\ \sqrt{\ }\ }\ \sigma_\phi\ \xrightarrow{\ \div(2\pi f_0)\ }\ \sigma_t.L(f)  ×2, de-dB ​ Sϕ​(f)  ∫f1​f2​​ ​ σϕ2​   ​ ​ σϕ​  ÷(2πf0​) ​ σt​.

We take each step apart, carrying units and a dimension check throughout, and finish with canonical Example C (5 GHz, −100-100−100 dBc/Hz @ 1 MHz, 1/f² slope, 1→100 MHz) to obtain σt=447.9\sigma_t=447.9σt​=447.9 fs.

Physical intuition (conclusion first): the phase error Δϕ\Delta\phiΔϕ is "how far the clock hand has strayed in angle"; divide that angle by the angular velocity 2πf02\pi f_02πf0​ and you get "how far the hand has strayed in time," Δt\Delta tΔt. The phase-noise plot tells you how much phase power density sits at each offset frequency; adding it all up (integrating) gives the total phase variance; the square root is the rms phase; dividing by 2πf02\pi f_02πf0​ gives the rms timing jitter. The whole plot is compressed into one fs number.

Step 1: why a phase error becomes a timing error​

An ideal oscillation cos⁡(2πf0t)\cos(2\pi f_0 t)cos(2πf0​t) becomes cos⁡(2πf0t+Δϕ)\cos(2\pi f_0 t+\Delta\phi)cos(2πf0​t+Δϕ) once excess phase is added. Factor the phase term out and look at where the zero crossing lands:

2πf0t+Δϕ=2πf0(t+Δϕ2πf0⏟= Δt).2\pi f_0 t+\Delta\phi=2\pi f_0\Big(t+\underbrace{\frac{\Delta\phi}{2\pi f_0}}_{=\ \Delta t}\Big).2πf0​t+Δϕ=2πf0​(t+= Δt2πf0​Δϕ​​​).

In other words, an extra phase Δϕ\Delta\phiΔϕ is equivalent to shifting the entire waveform along the time axis by Δt\Delta tΔt (spec §3, Eq. 17):

 Δt=Δϕ2πf0 \boxed{\ \Delta t=\frac{\Delta\phi}{2\pi f_0}\ } Δt=2πf0​Δϕ​ ​
  • Physics used: the conversion rate between phase and time is the angular frequency ω0=2πf0\omega_0=2\pi f_0ω0​=2πf0​ (rad/s).
  • Dimension check: [rad]/[rad/s]=[s][\text{rad}]/[\text{rad/s}]=[\text{s}][rad]/[rad/s]=[s] ✓. Note the denominator is 2πf02\pi f_02πf0​ (rad/s), not f0f_0f0​ (Hz) — dropping this 2π2\pi2π is the most common mistake.
  • Why it is reasonable: at small phase (Δϕ≪1\Delta\phi\ll1Δϕ≪1 rad), phase offset and edge-time offset are linear and one-to-one. Timing jitter is the time error of the zero crossing, so it equals Δϕ/(2πf0)\Delta\phi/(2\pi f_0)Δϕ/(2πf0​).
  • Feel: at 5 GHz, Δϕ=1\Delta\phi=1Δϕ=1 mrad ⇒Δt=31.8\Rightarrow\Delta t=31.8⇒Δt=31.8 fs (see numerical_feeling Example 1).

Step 2: converting dBc/Hz to linear and recovering the phase PSD​

The vertical axis of a phase-noise plot is L(f)\mathcal{L}(f)L(f), the SSB phase noise (single-sideband phase noise), in dBc/Hz — meaning "at offset fff, within a 1 Hz bandwidth, how many dB below the carrier the single-sideband noise power sits" (dBc = dB relative to carrier).

de-dB (from dB back to linear): dBc/Hz is 10log⁡10(⋅)10\log_{10}(\cdot)10log10​(⋅), so

Llin(f)=10L(f)/10[1/Hz].\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}(f)=10^{\mathcal{L}(f)/10}\quad[\text{1/Hz}].Llin​(f)=10L(f)/10[1/Hz].

Then connect to the phase PSD: under the small-angle approximation, the SSB phase noise relates to the single-sided phase PSD as (spec §3, Eq. 16):

 L(f)≈12 Sϕ(f) ⟹Sϕ(f)=2⋅10L(f)/10 [rad2/Hz].\boxed{\ \mathcal{L}(f)\approx\tfrac12\,S_\phi(f)\ }\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S_\phi(f)=2\cdot10^{\mathcal{L}(f)/10}\ [\text{rad}^2/\text{Hz}]. L(f)≈21​Sϕ​(f) ​⟹Sϕ​(f)=2⋅10L(f)/10 [rad2/Hz].
  • Units: SϕS_\phiSϕ​ is rad2/Hz\text{rad}^2/\text{Hz}rad2/Hz (the density of phase variance). Llin\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}Llin​ itself is dimensionless per Hz; after multiplying by 2 it is read as rad2/Hz\text{rad}^2/\text{Hz}rad2/Hz.
  • Where this 12\frac1221​ comes from: phase-modulation power splits evenly between the upper and lower sidebands, and L\mathcal{L}L counts only one side, so it is half of SϕS_\phiSϕ​. This is exactly the bookkeeping convention discussed in the spec §3 "factor-of-2 teaching note"; for jitter integrals this site always uses Sϕ=2LlinS_\phi=2\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}Sϕ​=2Llin​. For a deeper discussion see white_noise_to_phase_noise.

Step-by-step derivation: L≈12Sϕ\mathcal{L}\approx\tfrac12 S_\phiL≈21​Sϕ​ comes from small-angle (narrowband) PM​

That 12\frac1221​ above is not pulled out of thin air; it is a direct consequence of narrowband phase modulation (PM with a very small phase swing). We derive it step by step and make "one sideband's power =(ϕp/2)2=(\phi_p/2)^2=(ϕp​/2)2" explicit.

Step 1: write a carrier phase-modulated by a single tone. Let the phase be modulated at a single frequency ωm\omega_mωm​ (offset angular frequency) with a small amplitude ϕp\phi_pϕp​ (peak phase, the peak of the phase swing, rad):

v(t)=cos⁡(ω0t+ϕ(t)),ϕ(t)=ϕpsin⁡ωmt,ϕp≪1 rad.v(t)=\cos\big(\omega_0 t+\phi(t)\big),\qquad \phi(t)=\phi_p\sin\omega_m t,\quad \phi_p\ll 1\ \text{rad}.v(t)=cos(ω0​t+ϕ(t)),ϕ(t)=ϕp​sinωm​t,ϕp​≪1 rad.
  • Physics used: this is Step 1's excess phase Δϕ\Delta\phiΔϕ replaced by a phase that swings sinusoidally in time; ϕp\phi_pϕp​ is its amplitude.
  • Units: ϕp\phi_pϕp​ and ϕ(t)\phi(t)ϕ(t) are both rad; ω0,ωm\omega_0,\omega_mω0​,ωm​ are both rad/s.

Step 2: expand the phase modulation with a trig identity. Use the angle-sum formula cos⁡(A+B)=cos⁡Acos⁡B−sin⁡Asin⁡B\cos(A+B)=\cos A\cos B-\sin A\sin Bcos(A+B)=cosAcosB−sinAsinB with A=ω0tA=\omega_0 tA=ω0​t, B=ϕ(t)B=\phi(t)B=ϕ(t):

Step 3: the small-angle approximation (this is all "small-angle" means). Because ϕp≪1\phi_p\ll1ϕp​≪1:

cos⁡ ⁣(ϕpsin⁡ωmt)≈1,sin⁡ ⁣(ϕpsin⁡ωmt)≈ϕpsin⁡ωmt.\cos\!\big(\phi_p\sin\omega_m t\big)\approx 1,\qquad \sin\!\big(\phi_p\sin\omega_m t\big)\approx \phi_p\sin\omega_m t.cos(ϕp​sinωm​t)≈1,sin(ϕp​sinωm​t)≈ϕp​sinωm​t.
  • Math used: Taylor expansions cos⁡x≈1−x2/2\cos x\approx1-x^2/2cosx≈1−x2/2, sin⁡x≈x\sin x\approx xsinx≈x, keeping first order only (x=ϕpsin⁡ωmtx=\phi_p\sin\omega_m tx=ϕp​sinωm​t, whose x2x^2x2 is O(ϕp2)O(\phi_p^2)O(ϕp2​) and can be dropped). Substituting back:

v(t)≈cos⁡ω0t−ϕpsin⁡ω0t sin⁡ωmt.v(t)\approx\cos\omega_0 t-\phi_p\sin\omega_0 t\,\sin\omega_m t.v(t)≈cosω0​t−ϕp​sinω0​tsinωm​t.

Step 4: split sin⁡×sin⁡\sin\times\sinsin×sin into the two sidebands. Use the product-to-sum identity sin⁡αsin⁡β=12[cos⁡(α−β)−cos⁡(α+β)]\sin\alpha\sin\beta=\tfrac12[\cos(\alpha-\beta)-\cos(\alpha+\beta)]sinαsinβ=21​[cos(α−β)−cos(α+β)] with α=ω0t\alpha=\omega_0 tα=ω0​t, β=ωmt\beta=\omega_m tβ=ωm​t:

  • Physical meaning: phase modulation grows a symmetric pair of sidebands next to the carrier, at ω0±ωm\omega_0\pm\omega_mω0​±ωm​, each with amplitude ϕp/2\phi_p/2ϕp​/2. This is the time-domain origin of the "carrier smeared into a skirt" picture of [P1] Fig. 8.

Step 5: each sideband's relative power. The carrier has amplitude 111, power ∝1\propto 1∝1 (taking 12⋅12\tfrac12\cdot1^221​⋅12); a single sideband has amplitude ϕp/2\phi_p/2ϕp​/2, power ∝(ϕp/2)2\propto(\phi_p/2)^2∝(ϕp​/2)2. So the single-sideband-to-carrier power ratio is

P1 sidebandPcarrier=12(ϕp/2)212(1)2=(ϕp2)2=ϕp24.\frac{P_{\text{1 sideband}}}{P_{\text{carrier}}}=\frac{\tfrac12(\phi_p/2)^2}{\tfrac12(1)^2}=\Big(\frac{\phi_p}{2}\Big)^2=\frac{\phi_p^2}{4}.Pcarrier​P1 sideband​​=21​(1)221​(ϕp​/2)2​=(2ϕp​​)2=4ϕp2​​.
  • Dimension check: a power ratio is dimensionless ✓; ϕp\phi_pϕp​ (rad) squared is read as rad2\text{rad}^2rad2 in the "phase power" context.

Step 6: connect it to SϕS_\phiSϕ​. For ϕ(t)=ϕpsin⁡ωmt\phi(t)=\phi_p\sin\omega_m tϕ(t)=ϕp​sinωm​t, the mean square (variance) of the phase is

⟨ϕ2(t)⟩=ϕp2⟨sin⁡2ωmt⟩=ϕp22.\langle\phi^2(t)\rangle=\phi_p^2\langle\sin^2\omega_m t\rangle=\frac{\phi_p^2}{2}.⟨ϕ2(t)⟩=ϕp2​⟨sin2ωm​t⟩=2ϕp2​​.

All of this single tone's phase power ϕp2/2\phi_p^2/2ϕp2​/2 is concentrated in the one spectral line at ωm\omega_mωm​; reading it as "the single-sided phase-PSD strength at ωm\omega_mωm​" gives Sϕ(ωm)=ϕp2/2S_\phi(\omega_m)=\phi_p^2/2Sϕ​(ωm​)=ϕp2​/2 (per-Hz, taken as the weight of one line).

Step 7: divide the two, and the 12\frac1221​ appears. L\mathcal{L}L (SSB) = single-sideband power ratio =ϕp2/4=\phi_p^2/4=ϕp2​/4; Sϕ=ϕp2/2S_\phi=\phi_p^2/2Sϕ​=ϕp2​/2. So

 L=ϕp2/41=12⋅ϕp22=12Sϕ \boxed{\ \mathcal{L}=\frac{\phi_p^2/4}{1}=\frac12\cdot\frac{\phi_p^2}{2}=\frac12 S_\phi\ } L=1ϕp2​/4​=21​⋅2ϕp2​​=21​Sϕ​ ​
  • One-sentence summary: L\mathcal{L}L counts one sideband (power (ϕp/2)2=ϕp2/4(\phi_p/2)^2=\phi_p^2/4(ϕp​/2)2=ϕp2​/4), while SϕS_\phiSϕ​ is the total phase power density (ϕp2/2\phi_p^2/2ϕp2​/2, i.e. the two sidebands combined).   • Failure condition: once ϕp\phi_pϕp​ is no longer ≪1\ll1≪1, the higher-order Bessel terms of Step 3 (J2,J3,…J_2,J_3,\dotsJ2​,J3​,…) grow additional sidebands and L=12Sϕ\mathcal{L}=\tfrac12 S_\phiL=21​Sϕ​ no longer holds — at large phase the carrier also "loses power" to the higher-order sidebands.
  • Small-angle condition: L≈12Sϕ\mathcal{L}\approx\frac12 S_\phiL≈21​Sϕ​ holds only for σϕ≪1\sigma_\phi\ll1σϕ​≪1 rad (Bessel expansion kept to first order). Here σϕ=14\sigma_\phi=14σϕ​=14 mrad ≪1\ll1≪1, OK.
  • Canonical numbers: at 1 MHz, L=−100\mathcal{L}=-100L=−100 dBc/Hz: Llin=10−100/10=10−10\mathcal{L}_{\text{lin}}=10^{-100/10}=10^{-10}Llin​=10−100/10=10−10; Sϕ(1MHz)=2×10−10 rad2/HzS_\phi(1\text{MHz})=2\times10^{-10}\ \text{rad}^2/\text{Hz}Sϕ​(1MHz)=2×10−10 rad2/Hz.

Step 3: why integrating the phase PSD gives the variance​

This step is pure Parseval / Wiener–Khinchin: integrate the PSD over frequency to obtain the time-domain variance (spec §3, Eq. 18):

 σϕ2=∫f1f2Sϕ(f) df \boxed{\ \sigma_\phi^2=\int_{f_1}^{f_2}S_\phi(f)\,df\ } σϕ2​=∫f1​f2​​Sϕ​(f)df ​
  • Math used: Sϕ(f)S_\phi(f)Sϕ​(f) is "phase power per unit bandwidth"; summing (integrating) it over the offset band of interest [f1,f2][f_1,f_2][f1​,f2​] gives the total phase power = the variance σϕ2\sigma_\phi^2σϕ2​. This is the same move as integrating the current PSD into in2‾\overline{i_n^2}in2​​ in Section 3 of stochastic_noise_basics.
  • Dimension check: (rad2/Hz)×Hz=rad2(\text{rad}^2/\text{Hz})\times\text{Hz}=\text{rad}^2(rad2/Hz)×Hz=rad2 ✓.
  • Why the integration band [f1,f2][f_1,f_2][f1​,f2​] matters: the phase PSD at low offset is typically 1/f² (or even 1/f³), so the integral is dominated by the lower limit f1f_1f1​. Moving f1f_1f1​ by one decade can change the jitter by several times. So a reported jitter number must state its integration bandwidth, otherwise it is meaningless.
    • Upper limit f2f_2f2​: physically set by the system bandwidth (for SerDes, the PLL loop bandwidth or Nyquist).
    • Lower limit f1f_1f1​: for an open-loop oscillator the integral diverges toward DC (random walk); in practice it is set by the measurement time or by the frequency at which a PLL "holds" the phase.
  • Small-angle approximation (sin⁡Δϕ≈Δϕ\sin\Delta\phi\approx\Delta\phisinΔϕ≈Δϕ): approximating the phase-modulation power as σϕ2\sigma_\phi^2σϕ2​ likewise requires σϕ≪1\sigma_\phi\ll1σϕ​≪1 rad.

The closed-form 1/f² integral (the core of this example)​

Anchor the 1/f² shape from Step 2 at fref=1f_{ref}=1fref​=1 MHz:

Sϕ(f)=Sϕ(fref)(freff)2=2×10−10(106f)2.S_\phi(f)=S_\phi(f_{ref})\Big(\frac{f_{ref}}{f}\Big)^2=2\times10^{-10}\Big(\frac{10^6}{f}\Big)^2.Sϕ​(f)=Sϕ​(fref​)(ffref​​)2=2×10−10(f106​)2.

Take the square root:

σϕ=1.98×10−4=1.407×10−2 rad=14.07 mrad.\sigma_\phi=\sqrt{1.98\times10^{-4}}=1.407\times10^{-2}\ \text{rad}=14.07\ \text{mrad}.σϕ​=1.98×10−4​=1.407×10−2 rad=14.07 mrad.

Step 4: phase variance → rms jitter​

Apply Step 1's Δt=Δϕ/(2πf0)\Delta t=\Delta\phi/(2\pi f_0)Δt=Δϕ/(2πf0​) to the rms quantities (spec §3, Eq. 19):

 σt=σϕ2πf0=12πf0∫f1f2Sϕ(f) df \boxed{\ \sigma_t=\frac{\sigma_\phi}{2\pi f_0}=\frac{1}{2\pi f_0}\sqrt{\int_{f_1}^{f_2}S_\phi(f)\,df}\ } σt​=2πf0​σϕ​​=2πf0​1​∫f1​f2​​Sϕ​(f)df​ ​

The four "dialects" of jitter (using the notation page's table)​

The same word "jitter" can refer to entirely different measured quantities. The table below follows the definitions of notation (spec §2):

NameDefinitionIntuitionRelation to phase noise
random jitter (RJ)Gaussian, unbounded, described by σ\sigmaσwander from random kicksexactly the σt\sigma_tσt​ integrated above; SerDes BER uses it to estimate eye closure
period jitterTk−TT_k-TTk​−T (a single period relative to nominal)how long/short this one beat isintegrate SϕS_\phiSϕ​ after applying a sin⁡2(πf/f0)\sin^2(\pi f/f_0)sin2(πf/f0​)-type high-pass weight
cycle-to-cycle jitterTk+1−TkT_{k+1}-T_kTk+1​−Tk​ (difference of adjacent periods)how fast the beat changes beat to beatdifferences adjacent periods; even stronger high-pass weighting, least affected by close-in
accumulated / long-term jitteredge error over separation Δt\Delta tΔt, σΔt=κΔt\sigma_{\Delta t}=\kappa\sqrt{\Delta t}σΔt​=κΔt​an open-loop oscillator drifts further the longer it runsrandom walk; corresponds to integrating 1/f² down to very low offset ([P2] Eq.(8))
  • Why the distinction matters: period / cycle-to-cycle jitter apply a high-pass weight to SϕS_\phiSϕ​ (differencing suppresses low frequencies and amplifies high ones), so they are not dominated by close-in 1/f²; whereas the random / integrated jitter computed above (the absolute zero-crossing time error) is dominated by the lower limit. When reporting a number, say which kind it is, or the figures can differ by orders of magnitude.
  • accumulated jitter: a free-running oscillator has no absolute time reference; its phase is a random walk, σΔt=κΔt\sigma_{\Delta t}=\kappa\sqrt{\Delta t}σΔt​=κΔt​ ([P2] Eq.(8), p.792, claim C6). For ring details see [P2] and serdes_clocking_connection.

Weighting kernels for period / cycle-to-cycle / accumulated jitter (step-by-step derivation)​

The "high-pass weighting" in the table above is not hand-waving; it has exact kernels. Key idea: the different kinds of jitter are different "differences" of the same phase process ϕ(t)\phi(t)ϕ(t), and a time-domain difference is, in the frequency domain, multiplication by a transfer function. We derive the kernels step by step.

Step 1: write each edge's time error as a sample of the phase. The kkk-th edge nominally lands at tk=kTt_k=kTtk​=kT; its time error is the phase at that instant divided by the angular velocity (reusing Step 1's Δt=Δϕ/(2πf0)\Delta t=\Delta\phi/(2\pi f_0)Δt=Δϕ/(2πf0​)):

Δtk=ϕ(kT)2πf0.\Delta t_k=\frac{\phi(kT)}{2\pi f_0}.Δtk​=2πf0​ϕ(kT)​.

Step 2: three jitters = three differences. By definition (spec §2):

period jitter:Jkper=Δtk+1−Δtk=ϕ((k+1)T)−ϕ(kT)2πf0(first difference of the phase),cycle-to-cycle:Jkc2c=Jk+1per−Jkper(second difference of the phase),accumulated:Δtk=ϕ(kT)2πf0(no differencing; the phase itself).\begin{aligned} \text{period jitter:}\quad &J^{\text{per}}_k=\Delta t_{k+1}-\Delta t_k=\frac{\phi((k{+}1)T)-\phi(kT)}{2\pi f_0}\quad(\text{first difference of the phase}),\\ \text{cycle-to-cycle:}\quad &J^{\text{c2c}}_k=J^{\text{per}}_{k+1}-J^{\text{per}}_k\quad(\text{second difference of the phase}),\\ \text{accumulated:}\quad &\Delta t_k=\frac{\phi(kT)}{2\pi f_0}\quad(\text{no differencing; the phase itself}). \end{aligned}period jitter:cycle-to-cycle:accumulated:​Jkper​=Δtk+1​−Δtk​=2πf0​ϕ((k+1)T)−ϕ(kT)​(first difference of the phase),Jkc2c​=Jk+1per​−Jkper​(second difference of the phase),Δtk​=2πf0​ϕ(kT)​(no differencing; the phase itself).​

Step 3: differencing in the frequency domain = multiplying by (1−e−j2πfT)(1-e^{-j2\pi fT})(1−e−j2πfT). For a frequency component ϕ(t)∝ej2πft\phi(t)\propto e^{j2\pi ft}ϕ(t)∝ej2πft, a delay of one period TTT is multiplication by e−j2πfTe^{-j2\pi fT}e−j2πfT. So the frequency response of the first-difference operator "now minus one period ago" is

Hper(f)=1−e−j2πfT,∣Hper(f)∣2=∣1−e−j2πfT∣2=4sin⁡2(πfT).H_{\text{per}}(f)=1-e^{-j2\pi fT},\qquad \lvert H_{\text{per}}(f)\rvert^2=\big\lvert 1-e^{-j2\pi fT}\big\rvert^2=4\sin^2(\pi fT).Hper​(f)=1−e−j2πfT,∣Hper​(f)∣2=​1−e−j2πfT​2=4sin2(πfT).
  • Algebraic expansion: ∣1−e−jθ∣2=(1−cos⁡θ)2+sin⁡2θ=2−2cos⁡θ=4sin⁡2(θ/2)\lvert 1-e^{-j\theta}\rvert^2=(1-\cos\theta)^2+\sin^2\theta=2-2\cos\theta=4\sin^2(\theta/2)∣1−e−jθ∣2=(1−cosθ)2+sin2θ=2−2cosθ=4sin2(θ/2); substituting θ=2πfT\theta=2\pi fTθ=2πfT gives 4sin⁡2(πfT)4\sin^2(\pi fT)4sin2(πfT).
  • Why it is high-pass: as f→0f\to0f→0, sin⁡2(πfT)≈(πfT)2→0\sin^2(\pi fT)\approx(\pi fT)^2\to0sin2(πfT)≈(πfT)2→0 — low frequencies get crushed; the maximum of 444 occurs at f=f0/2=1/(2T)f=f_0/2=1/(2T)f=f0​/2=1/(2T). This is the mathematical reason period jitter is "not dominated by close-in 1/f²".

Step 4: put the kernel into the phase-variance integral. Multiply the phase-variance density by ∣H∣2\lvert H\rvert^2∣H∣2, integrate, then divide by (2πf0)2(2\pi f_0)^2(2πf0​)2 to convert to time (spec §10.2, period/cycle-to-cycle jitter kernels):

Cycle-to-cycle takes one more difference, so the kernel is squared once more (a second difference = the first-difference operator applied twice):

σcc2=1(2πf0)2∫0∞Sϕ(f) ∣1−e−j2πfT∣4 df=1(2πf0)2∫0∞Sϕ(f) 16sin⁡4(πfT) df.\sigma_{cc}^2=\frac{1}{(2\pi f_0)^2}\int_0^{\infty}S_\phi(f)\,\big\lvert 1-e^{-j2\pi fT}\big\rvert^4\,df =\frac{1}{(2\pi f_0)^2}\int_0^{\infty}S_\phi(f)\,16\sin^4(\pi fT)\,df.σcc2​=(2πf0​)21​∫0∞​Sϕ​(f)​1−e−j2πfT​4df=(2πf0​)21​∫0∞​Sϕ​(f)16sin4(πfT)df.

Accumulated jitter has no differencing kernel (kernel =1=1=1), so it is dominated by low frequencies (the 1/f² integral diverges once carried to very low offset), which is precisely the frequency-domain counterpart of its Δt\sqrt{\Delta t}Δt​ random-walk growth ([P2] Eq.(8), p.792; κ from Eq.(12), p.793).

  • Dimension check: SϕS_\phiSϕ​ (rad2/Hz\text{rad}^2/\text{Hz}rad2/Hz) × dimensionless kernel × Hz\text{Hz}Hz = rad2\text{rad}^2rad2; dividing by (2πf0)2(2\pi f_0)^2(2πf0​)2 (rad2/s2\text{rad}^2/\text{s}^2rad2/s2) gives s2\text{s}^2s2 ✓, and the square root yields seconds.
  • Three-line comparison: accumulated kernel =1=1=1 (low-frequency dominated); period kernel ∣1−e−j2πfT∣2\lvert1-e^{-j2\pi fT}\rvert^2∣1−e−j2πfT∣2 (first-order high-pass); cycle-to-cycle kernel ∣1−e−j2πfT∣4\lvert1-e^{-j2\pi fT}\rvert^4∣1−e−j2πfT∣4 (second-order high-pass, least sensitive to close-in).

  • Weighting kernels: accumulated kernel =1=1=1 (low-frequency dominated); period kernel ∣1−e−j2πfT∣2=4sin⁡2(πfT)\lvert1-e^{-j2\pi fT}\rvert^2=4\sin^2(\pi fT)∣1−e−j2πfT∣2=4sin2(πfT) (first-order high-pass); cycle-to-cycle kernel ∣1−e−j2πfT∣4\lvert1-e^{-j2\pi fT}\rvert^4∣1−e−j2πfT∣4 (second-order high-pass).
  • Same −100-100−100 dBc/Hz plot: RJ σt=447.9\sigma_t=447.9σt​=447.9 fs, but the period jitter is only ∼5.6\sim5.6∼5.6 fs — the high-pass kernel crushes close-in.
