Flicker noise upconversion into 1/f³ phase noise

Prerequisites: white_noise_to_phase_noise (the same mechanism, white noise → 1/f21/f^21/f2), fourier_series_of_isf (the role of the DC term c0c_0c0​), rms_isf (the c0c_0c0​-to-Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​ ratio sets the corner).

Hands-on verification: this page's "symmetric vs asymmetric waveforms decide close-in 1/f31/f^31/f3" simulation is in lab_07.

The previous page white_noise_to_phase_noise explained how white noise turns into 1/f21/f^21/f2. But look at any real oscillator's phase-noise plot: the segment closest to the carrier is usually steeper — slope −30-30−30 dB/decade, i.e. 1/f31/f^31/f3. Where does this steep skirt come from? Answer: device flicker noise (1/f1/f1/f noise, the noise that is especially strong in transistors at low frequency) gets "upconverted" by the oscillator to the vicinity of the carrier.

This page answers three things: (1) what flicker noise is, (2) why only the ISF's DC term c0c_0c0​ can upconvert it, (3) why the 1/f31/f^31/f3 corner is not equal to the device's 1/f1/f1/f corner, and how waveform symmetry helps.

Physical intuition (conclusion first): low-frequency flicker noise itself lives at baseband (near DC). For it to show up near the carrier, some mechanism must "move" it up to ω0\omega_0ω0​. The ISF is a periodic function, and in its Fourier series the only DC component is c0/2c_0/2c0​/2. Only this DC term multiplies the "flicker sitting right next to DC" and gets accumulated by the phase integrator into close-in phase jitter. In other words: c0c_0c0​ is flicker's only gate to the carrier. Push c0c_0c0​ to 0 (via waveform symmetry) and that gate closes — the 1/f31/f^31/f3 skirt drops dramatically.

Step 1: what flicker noise is, and how to model it​

Flicker (1/f1/f1/f) noise is the transistor's intrinsic low-frequency noise; its PSD rises toward low frequency (∝1/f\propto 1/f∝1/f), usually attributed to carrier trapping/release at the channel–oxide interface. Below the device's 1/f1/f1/f corner ω1/f\omega_{1/f}ω1/f​, flicker exceeds white noise; above it, white noise dominates. [P1] describes it with a compact model ([P1] Eq.(22), p.185):

in,1/f2‾=in2‾⋅ω1/fΔω(Δω<ω1/f)\overline{i_{n,1/f}^2}=\overline{i_n^2}\cdot\frac{\omega_{1/f}}{\Delta\omega}\qquad(\Delta\omega<\omega_{1/f})in,1/f2​​=in2​​⋅Δωω1/f​​(Δω<ω1/f​)
  • How to read it: in2‾\overline{i_n^2}in2​​ is the white-noise floor (per-Hz); multiplied by ω1/f/Δω\omega_{1/f}/\Delta\omegaω1/f​/Δω, it is amplified when Δω<ω1/f\Delta\omega<\omega_{1/f}Δω<ω1/f​ and rises as 1/Δω1/\Delta\omega1/Δω — exactly the 1/f1/f1/f shape. At Δω=ω1/f\Delta\omega=\omega_{1/f}Δω=ω1/f​ the two are equal (the definition of the corner).
  • Unit check: ω1/f/Δω\omega_{1/f}/\Delta\omegaω1/f​/Δω is dimensionless (rad/s divided by rad/s); multiplying in2‾\overline{i_n^2}in2​​ (A2/Hz\text{A}^2/\text{Hz}A2/Hz) still gives A2/Hz\text{A}^2/\text{Hz}A2/Hz ✓.
  • Watch the notation: ω1/f\omega_{1/f}ω1/f​ is the device 1/f1/f1/f corner (rad/s), set by the transistor process — a different thing from the phase-noise 1/f31/f^31/f3 corner that appears later (see Step 4; the notation page warns about this explicitly).

Step 2: why low-frequency noise must be "upconverted" to be visible​

Return to the harmonic-decomposition phase response of [P1] Eq.(13), p.183:

ϕ(t)=1qmax ⁣[c02 ⁣∫−∞t ⁣in dτ+∑n=1∞cn ⁣∫−∞t ⁣incos⁡(nω0τ+θn) dτ].\phi(t)=\frac{1}{q_{max}}\!\left[\frac{c_0}{2}\!\int_{-\infty}^{t}\!i_n\,d\tau+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n\!\int_{-\infty}^{t}\!i_n\cos(n\omega_0\tau+\theta_n)\,d\tau\right].ϕ(t)=qmax​1​[2c0​​∫−∞t​in​dτ+n=1∑∞​cn​∫−∞t​in​cos(nω0​τ+θn​)dτ].

Flicker's energy is concentrated at low frequency (near DC). Look at each term in the sum:

  • Every n≥1n\ge1n≥1 term carries cos⁡(nω0τ+θn)\cos(n\omega_0\tau+\theta_n)cos(nω0​τ+θn​) — it downconverts noise sitting "right around nω0n\omega_0nω0​" to baseband. But flicker has almost no energy at nω0n\omega_0nω0​ (≥ω0\ge\omega_0≥ω0​, which is high), so these terms cannot capture flicker.

  • Only the n=0n=0n=0 (DC) term c02∫in dτ\dfrac{c_0}{2}\int i_n\,d\tau2c0​​∫in​dτ has no cos⁡\coscos multiplier — it directly integrates baseband noise. Flicker's energy is exactly at baseband, so only this term accumulates flicker into phase.

  • Math used: frequency translation (mixing). cos⁡(nω0t)×\cos(n\omega_0 t)\timescos(nω0​t)×noise moves noise to ±nω0\pm n\omega_0±nω0​; only the DC multiplier (=1=1=1) leaves baseband noise at baseband to be integrated.

  • Physical meaning (claim C4): c0c_0c0​ (the ISF's DC Fourier coefficient) is the only channel from flicker to close-in. With c0=0c_0=0c0​=0, flicker simply never gets up there and the 1/f31/f^31/f3 skirt vanishes.

Step 3: deriving the 1/f³ phase noise (step-by-step algebra, Eq.(22)→(23))​

This step "multiplies" three things together: (i) flicker injected via c0c_0c0​, (ii) the 1/f21/f^21/f2 mechanism (the integrator), (iii) and the product is 1/f31/f^31/f3. No skipped steps — we compute line by line.

Step 3.1: the white-noise sum keeps only the DC term. The white-noise result (spec Section 3, formula 10; [P1] Eq.(19), p.185) is

L{Δω}=10log⁡10 ⁣((in2‾/Δf) ∑n=0∞cn28 qmax2 Δω2).\mathcal{L}\{\Delta\omega\}=10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{(\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f)\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n^2}{8\,q_{max}^2\,\Delta\omega^2}\right).L{Δω}=10log10​(8qmax2​Δω2(in2​​/Δf)∑n=0∞​cn2​​).

Flicker's energy is only at baseband; from Step 2, only the n=0n=0n=0 (DC) term can capture it (the remaining n≥1n\ge1n≥1 terms carry cos⁡(nω0τ)\cos(n\omega_0\tau)cos(nω0​τ), moving the noise to nω0n\omega_0nω0​, where flicker has no energy). So for flicker, the sum ∑n=0∞cn2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n^2∑n=0∞​cn2​ collapses to the single term c02c_0^2c02​:

∑n=0∞cn2 → flicker 只剩 DC  c02.\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n^2\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{flicker 只剩 DC}\ }\ c_0^2.n=0∑∞​cn2​  flicker 只剩 DC ​ c02​.
  • Why this c02c_0^2c02​ pairs with the 888 in the denominator: Eq.(19)'s denominator is 8qmax2Δω28q_{max}^2\Delta\omega^28qmax2​Δω2 and its numerator carries ∑cn2\sum c_n^2∑cn2​. Substituting gives the "DC-only, still white-noise" intermediate form:
L{Δω}∣1/f2, DC only=10log⁡10 ⁣(c02qmax2⋅in2‾/Δf8 Δω2).\mathcal{L}\{\Delta\omega\}\Big|_{1/f^2,\,\text{DC only}}=10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{c_0^2}{q_{max}^2}\cdot\frac{\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f}{8\,\Delta\omega^2}\right).L{Δω}​1/f2,DC only​=10log10​(qmax2​c02​​⋅8Δω2in2​​/Δf​).

Step 3.2: replace the white-noise floor with flicker (multiply by the amplification factor of Eq.(22)). The in2‾/Δf\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta fin2​​/Δf in the numerator above is still the white-noise floor. Device flicker ([P1] Eq.(22), p.185) says that for Δω<ω1/f\Delta\omega<\omega_{1/f}Δω<ω1/f​, the current noise is amplified by ω1/f/Δω\omega_{1/f}/\Delta\omegaω1/f​/Δω:

in,1/f2‾=in2‾⋅ω1/fΔω.\overline{i_{n,1/f}^2}=\overline{i_n^2}\cdot\frac{\omega_{1/f}}{\Delta\omega}.in,1/f2​​=in2​​⋅Δωω1/f​​.

Step 3.3: combine to get [P1] Eq.(23). Multiplying the two brackets and putting back the 10log⁡1010\log_{10}10log10​ yields the flicker-upconverted 1/f31/f^31/f3 phase noise ([P1] Eq.(23), p.185):

 L{Δω}=10log⁡10 ⁣(c02qmax2⋅in2‾/Δf8 Δω2⋅ω1/fΔω) \boxed{\ \mathcal{L}\{\Delta\omega\}=10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{c_0^2}{q_{max}^2}\cdot\frac{\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f}{8\,\Delta\omega^2}\cdot\frac{\omega_{1/f}}{\Delta\omega}\right)\ } L{Δω}=10log10​(qmax2​c02​​⋅8Δω2in2​​/Δf​⋅Δωω1/f​​) ​
  • See the 1/f31/f^31/f3? The denominator has Δω2\Delta\omega^2Δω2 (the integrator, 1/f21/f^21/f2) times one more Δω\Delta\omegaΔω (flicker's 1/Δω1/\Delta\omega1/Δω), together Δω3\Delta\omega^3Δω3. Step 4: the 1/f³ corner (Eq.(24)) — it is not the device 1/f corner​

On the phase-noise spectrum, the offset where the 1/f31/f^31/f3 segment meets the 1/f21/f^21/f2 segment is called the 1/f31/f^31/f3 corner Δω1/f3\Delta\omega_{1/f^3}Δω1/f3​. Set the 1/f31/f^31/f3 expression (Eq.(23), with c02c_0^2c02​) equal to the 1/f21/f^21/f2 expression (Eq.(21), with Γrms2\Gamma_{rms}^2Γrms2​) and solve for the intersection ([P1] Eq.(24), p.185):

 Δω1/f3=ω1/f⋅c022 Γrms2≈ω1/f(c0c1)2 \boxed{\ \Delta\omega_{1/f^3}=\omega_{1/f}\cdot\frac{c_0^2}{2\,\Gamma_{rms}^2}\approx\omega_{1/f}\left(\frac{c_0}{c_1}\right)^2\ } Δω1/f3​=ω1/f​⋅2Γrms2​c02​​≈ω1/f​(c1​c0​​)2 ​

  • The most important concept on this page (claim C5): Δω1/f3≠ω1/f\Delta\omega_{1/f^3}\ne\omega_{1/f}Δω1/f3​=ω1/f​.   • Where ≈ω1/f(c0/c1)2\approx\omega_{1/f}(c_0/c_1)^2≈ω1/f​(c0​/c1​)2 comes from: when the ISF is fundamental-dominated, Γrms2≈c12/2\Gamma_{rms}^2\approx c_1^2/2Γrms2​≈c12​/2 (Parseval keeps only the n=1n=1n=1 term); substituting gives the right-hand form.   • Unit check: ω1/f\omega_{1/f}ω1/f​ (rad/s) times a dimensionless ratio → rad/s ✓, an angular frequency.

Step 5: why waveform symmetry determines c0c_0c0​​

c0c_0c0​ is the ISF's DC Fourier coefficient; the ISF's DC value is c0/2c_0/2c0​/2 (the notation trap flagged on the notation page). The DC value is the ISF's average over one period. [P1] points out in the design section (p.187–188, Fig. 16):

The DC value of the ISF is determined by the waveform's symmetry, in particular its rise/fall symmetry. If the rise time and fall time differ significantly, the ISF has a large DC value (large c0c_0c0​).

Intuition: the ISF swings positive and negative in the "sensitive region" (near the waveform transitions). If the rising and falling segments are mirror-symmetric, the positive and negative swings cancel and the average ≈0\approx0≈0 (small c0c_0c0​); if they are asymmetric (e.g. fast rise, slow fall), they do not cancel, the average is nonzero (large c0c_0c0​), and flicker's gate opens wide.

  • Odd-symmetric waveforms (odd-symmetric, e.g. an ideal −sin⁡-\sin−sin, antiphase over half a period) have c0=0c_0=0c0​=0 — an excellent special case; but [P1] clarifies explicitly: small c0c_0c0​ is not limited to odd-symmetric waveforms — rise/fall symmetry alone suffices, a much broader class.
  • Toy contrast: lab_05 uses Γ=cos⁡θ\Gamma=\cos\thetaΓ=cosθ (symmetric, c0=0c_0=0c0​=0) against Γ=cos⁡θ+0.4\Gamma=\cos\theta+0.4Γ=cosθ+0.4 (deliberately added DC, c0=0.8c_0=0.8c0​=0.8) to show directly whether the DC term is present.

Step 6: how differential / complementary waveforms help — and their limits​

In practice two tricks are commonly used to approach symmetry and suppress c0c_0c0​:

  • Differential: use a pair of complementary nodes to cancel even harmonics and common-mode error, improving symmetry.
  • Complementary (complementary CMOS, symmetric PMOS/NMOS arrangement): deliberately match pull-up/pull-down so that rise/fall times are equal → rise/fall symmetry → small c0c_0c0​.

[P2] (the ring-oscillator paper) confirms this rule directly by experiment: phase noise varies with the "symmetry control voltage" and reaches a minimum at the symmetry point, and the 1/f31/f^31/f3 corner drops sharply at the symmetry point ([P2] Fig. 17, p.802; corresponds to claim C4).

Limits (honesty note) — [P2] also points out (Sec. VII Design Implications, p.798, original text):

  • Differential symmetry is not necessarily enough: [P2] states plainly that "differential symmetry is insufficient"; what is needed is rise/fall symmetry within each half period, not merely symmetry between the two differential branches.
  • The tail / bias source is a major leak: the ISF of the tail current source often has a large DC value, strongly upconverting the tail's flicker, which frequently dominates close-in noise. Symmetrizing the main signal path does not help — the tail must be handled separately.
  • More linear loads help: [P2] recommends more linear loads (e.g. resistors or long-channel devices) to make the waveform more symmetric and push the corner further down.
  • Even so, symmetry only suppresses flicker (1/f31/f^31/f3); it does not change the white-noise 1/f21/f^21/f2 region (that region is set by Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​, not c0c_0c0​). Do not expect symmetry to rescue the whole curve.

  • Feel for it: the device 1/f1/f1/f corner is 1 MHz in both cases, but the phase-noise 1/f31/f^31/f3 corner drops from 320 kHz (asymmetric) to 3.2 kHz (symmetric) — a full 100× lower (because c02c_0^2c02​ differs by 100×). This is the numerical picture of "1/f31/f^31/f3 corner ≠ device 1/f1/f1/f corner": good symmetry pushes the steep skirt in very close to the carrier, leaving the close-in region much cleaner.
  • Dimension check: the corner is a frequency; MHz×\text{MHz}\timesMHz× (dimensionless ratio) === frequency ✓.

Corresponding simulation plot (toy model, not transistor-level)​

lab_07 feeds flicker current into a symmetric (cos⁡\coscos, c0=0c_0=0c0​=0) and an asymmetric (cos⁡+0.5\cos+0.5cos+0.5) toy ISF and estimates the close-in phase PSD: the symmetric case shows almost no 1/f31/f^31/f3, while the asymmetric case shows a clear −30-30−30 dB/decade skirt. Core Python (full script: simulations/lab_07_flicker_noise.py):

import numpy as np
from simulations.common.noise_utils import flicker_noise, estimate_psd
from simulations.common.isf_utils import gamma_symmetric, gamma_asymmetric

fs, n, qmax = 256.0, 2**20, 1.0
t = np.arange(n) / fs
theta = 2 * np.pi * 1.0 * t                          # f0 = 1 (toy normalized frequency)

i_f = flicker_noise(n, fs, k_flicker=1e-4)           # 1/f current

# ISF weighting + integrator: phi = cumsum(Gamma * i_n / qmax) / fs
def phase_from_isf(i_n, gamma_vals, q_max, fs):
    g = gamma_vals * i_n / q_max
    return np.cumsum(g) / fs

gamma_sym  = gamma_symmetric(theta)                  # c0 = 0
gamma_asym = gamma_asymmetric(theta, alpha=0.5)      # c0 = 2*alpha = 1.0 (DC = 0.5)

phi_sym  = phase_from_isf(i_f, gamma_sym,  qmax, fs)  # symmetric -> close-in nearly flat
phi_asym = phase_from_isf(i_f, gamma_asym, qmax, fs)  # asymmetric -> 1/f^3 skirt

Example F: how symmetrization (small c0c_0c0​) pushes the corner down​

Problem: symmetrize the waveform of Example E so that c0c_0c0​ drops from 0.4 to 0.04 (canonical Γrms=0.5\Gamma_{rms}=0.5Γrms​=0.5 and f1/f=1f_{1/f}=1f1/f​=1 MHz unchanged). Find the new f1/f3f_{1/f^3}f1/f3​ and explain how symmetry does its work.

Step 1 (new ratio): c022Γrms2=0.0422×0.52=1.6×10−30.5=3.2×10−3\dfrac{c_0^2}{2\Gamma_{rms}^2}=\dfrac{0.04^2}{2\times0.5^2}=\dfrac{1.6\times10^{-3}}{0.5}=3.2\times10^{-3}2Γrms2​c02​​=2×0.520.042​=0.51.6×10−3​=3.2×10−3.

Step 2 (multiply by the device corner):

f1/f3=1 MHz×3.2×10−3=3.2 kHz.f_{1/f^3}=1\,\text{MHz}\times3.2\times10^{-3}=3.2\ \text{kHz}.f1/f3​=1MHz×3.2×10−3=3.2 kHz.
  • Result: f1/f3=3.2f_{1/f^3}=3.2f1/f3​=3.2 kHz. A 10× drop in c0c_0c0​ ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ a 100× drop in c02c_0^2c02​ ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ the corner falls from 320 kHz to 3.2 kHz (100× lower).
  • How symmetry pushes it down (the point of this problem): corner ∝c02\propto c_0^2∝c02​, and c0/2c_0/2c0​/2 is the ISF's average over one period (the DC value). Make the waveform's rise/fall symmetric, and the ISF's positive and negative swings in the sensitive region cancel → c0c_0c0​ approaches 0 → the corner collapses at a quadratic rate. This is the numerical picture of "1/f31/f^31/f3 corner ≠ device 1/f1/f1/f corner": the device corner is still 1 MHz, but the steep close-in skirt is pushed down to 3.2 kHz, leaving the carrier's vicinity much cleaner.
  • Dimension check: Hz × dimensionless = Hz ✓.
# symmetrization shrinks c0 -> corner ∝ c0^2 collapses quadratically (canonical Gamma_rms=0.5)
f_1f = 1e6; gamma_rms_val = 0.5
for c0 in (0.4, 0.04):
    print(c0, "->", f_1f*c0**2/(2*gamma_rms_val**2)/1e3, "kHz")   # 0.4 -> 320.0 kHz ; 0.04 -> 3.2 kHz

Key takeaways​

  • Flicker is the device's low-frequency noise (∝1/f\propto1/f∝1/f); it must be "upconverted" to appear near the carrier.
  • Only the ISF's DC term c0c_0c0​ can upconvert flicker → close-in becomes −30-30−30 dB/decade 1/f31/f^31/f3 (Eq.23).
  • 1/f31/f^31/f3 corner =ω1/f⋅c02/(2Γrms2)≠=\omega_{1/f}\cdot c_0^2/(2\Gamma_{rms}^2)\ne=ω1/f​⋅c02​/(2Γrms2​)= device 1/f1/f1/f corner (Eq.24); a symmetric waveform (small c0c_0c0​) pushes the corner far below the device corner.
  • Waveform rise/fall symmetry ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ ISF average (c0/2c_0/2c0​/2) ≈0\approx0≈0 ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ 1/f31/f^31/f3 drops dramatically.
  • Differential/complementary designs help symmetry, but differential symmetry alone is not enough, the tail source's large c0c_0c0​ is a leak, and symmetry does not improve the white-noise 1/f21/f^21/f2 region.
  • Numbers: with a 1 MHz device corner and canonical Γrms=0.5\Gamma_{rms}=0.5Γrms​=0.5, moving c0c_0c0​ from 0.4→0.04 takes the 1/f31/f^31/f3 corner from 320 kHz→3.2 kHz (a factor of 100).
