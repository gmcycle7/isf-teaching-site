The rms ISF and the Parseval Relation

β: This English translation is in beta — the Traditional-Chinese original is the authoritative version.

Prerequisites: fourier_series_of_isf (the cnc_ncn​ coefficients of Γ\GammaΓ), stochastic_noise_basics (Parseval / power spectra), convolution_derivation (the phase integral).

The previous page, fourier_series_of_isf, decomposed the ISF into a set of Fourier coefficients c0,c1,c2,…c_0,c_1,c_2,\dotsc0​,c1​,c2​,… and showed that each cnc_ncn​ folds the noise near nω0n\omega_0nω0​ back onto the carrier. This page answers:

When the device noise is flat white noise and the contributions of all bands must be summed, can a single number describe the entire ISF's contribution to phase noise? It can — that number is the rms value of the ISF, Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​, tied to the sum of squared coefficients by Parseval's theorem ([P1] Eq.(20), p.185):

∑n=0∞cn2=1π∫02π∣Γ(x)∣2dx=2 Γrms2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n^2=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|\Gamma(x)|^2dx=2\,\Gamma_{rms}^2n=0∑∞​cn2​=π1​∫02π​∣Γ(x)∣2dx=2Γrms2​

Physical intuition (conclusion first): white noise is equally strong in every nω0n\omega_0nω0​ band, so the "total folded-back power" is proportional to the sum of the squares of all coefficients, ∑cn2\sum c_n^2∑cn2​. Parseval tells us this sum equals twice the mean square of the ISF over one period, i.e. 2Γrms22\Gamma_{rms}^22Γrms2​. The messy band-by-band summation thus collapses into one clean shape metric, Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​ — which (together with qmaxq_{max}qmax​) sets the level of the 1/f21/f^21/f2 phase noise. Intuitively: the "quieter" the ISF is overall (the smaller its rms), the less sensitive the oscillator is to white noise.

Step 1: Parseval's theorem — time-domain energy = sum of squared frequency-domain coefficients​

Parseval's theorem states: the mean square of a periodic function over one period equals the sum of the mean squares of its Fourier components. For the standard cos/sin expansion Γ(x)=a02+∑n≥1[ancos⁡nx+bnsin⁡nx]\Gamma(x)=\dfrac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n\ge1}[a_n\cos nx+b_n\sin nx]Γ(x)=2a0​​+∑n≥1​[an​cosnx+bn​sinnx]:

12π∫02π∣Γ(x)∣2 dx=(a02)2+12∑n=1∞(an2+bn2).\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|\Gamma(x)|^2\,dx =\left(\frac{a_0}{2}\right)^2+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_n^2+b_n^2).2π1​∫02π​∣Γ(x)∣2dx=(2a0​​)2+21​n=1∑∞​(an2​+bn2​).
  • Math used: the basis {1,cos⁡nx,sin⁡nx}\{1,\cos nx,\sin nx\}{1,cosnx,sinnx} is orthogonal. After expanding ∣Γ∣2|\Gamma|^2∣Γ∣2, all cross terms such as ∫cos⁡mxcos⁡nx dx\int\cos mx\cos nx\,dx∫cosmxcosnxdx (m≠nm\neq nm=n) integrate to 0; only the "self-with-self" terms survive.
  • Where each term's coefficient comes from: 12π∫02πcos⁡2(nx) dx=12\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2(nx)\,dx=\dfrac122π1​∫02π​cos2(nx)dx=21​ (n≥1n\ge1n≥1), so each ancos⁡nxa_n\cos nxan​cosnx contributes 12an2\dfrac12 a_n^221​an2​ to the mean square; the constant term a02\dfrac{a_0}{2}2a0​​ has mean square (a02)2\left(\dfrac{a_0}{2}\right)^2(2a0​​)2 (the mean square of a constant is its own square — no 12\frac1221​). This factor difference between the DC and AC terms is the single most error-prone spot in the entire derivation; Step 4 is devoted to it.

Step-by-step algebra (killing the cross terms one at a time, no skipped steps): first expand the square of Γ\GammaΓ in full, then integrate class by class.

12π∫02π∣Γ∣2dx=12π∫02π ⁣[a02+∑m≥1(amcos⁡mx+bmsin⁡mx)]2dx=12π∫02π(a02)2dx⏟(I) DC×DC+12π∫02π2⋅a02∑m≥1(amcos⁡mx+bmsin⁡mx) dx⏟(II) DC×AC+12π∫02π[∑m≥1(amcos⁡mx+bmsin⁡mx)]2dx⏟(III) AC×AC.\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|\Gamma|^2dx &=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\left[\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{m\ge1}(a_m\cos mx+b_m\sin mx)\right]^2 dx\\ &=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\left(\frac{a_0}{2}\right)^2 dx}_{\text{(I) DC×DC}} +\underbrace{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}2\cdot\frac{a_0}{2}\sum_{m\ge1}(a_m\cos mx+b_m\sin mx)\,dx}_{\text{(II) DC×AC}}\\ &\quad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\Big[\sum_{m\ge1}(a_m\cos mx+b_m\sin mx)\Big]^2 dx}_{\text{(III) AC×AC}}. \end{aligned}2π1​∫02π​∣Γ∣2dx​=2π1​∫02π​[2a0​​+m≥1∑​(am​cosmx+bm​sinmx)]2dx=(I) DC×DC2π1​∫02π​(2a0​​)2dx​​+(II) DC×AC2π1​∫02π​2⋅2a0​​m≥1∑​(am​cosmx+bm​sinmx)dx​​+(III) AC×AC2π1​∫02π​[m≥1∑​(am​cosmx+bm​sinmx)]2dx​​.​

Term by term (with the orthogonality used at each step called out):

  • (I): the integrand is a constant, 12π∫02π(a02)2dx=(a02)2\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\left(\dfrac{a_0}{2}\right)^2dx=\left(\dfrac{a_0}{2}\right)^22π1​∫02π​(2a0​​)2dx=(2a0​​)2.
  • (II): every ∫02πcos⁡mx dx=∫02πsin⁡mx dx=0\int_0^{2\pi}\cos mx\,dx=\int_0^{2\pi}\sin mx\,dx=0∫02π​cosmxdx=∫02π​sinmxdx=0 (m≥1m\ge1m≥1), so the whole block = 0. This is "the constant is orthogonal to every harmonic."
  • (III): expanding the square produces three kinds of integrals. First, ∫02πcos⁡mxcos⁡nx dx=π δmn\int_0^{2\pi}\cos mx\cos nx\,dx=\pi\,\delta_{mn}∫02π​cosmxcosnxdx=πδmn​ and ∫02πsin⁡mxsin⁡nx dx=π δmn\int_0^{2\pi}\sin mx\sin nx\,dx=\pi\,\delta_{mn}∫02π​sinmxsinnxdx=πδmn​ (m,n≥1m,n\ge1m,n≥1); second, ∫02πcos⁡mxsin⁡nx dx=0\int_0^{2\pi}\cos mx\sin nx\,dx=0∫02π​cosmxsinnxdx=0 (all of these vanish — cos and sin are mutually orthogonal). Only the m=nm=nm=n "self-with-self" terms survive, each weighted 12π⋅π=12\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\pi=\dfrac122π1​⋅π=21​:
(III)=12∑n≥1(an2+bn2).\text{(III)}=\frac12\sum_{n\ge1}(a_n^2+b_n^2).(III)=21​n≥1∑​(an2​+bn2​).

Collecting (I)+(II)+(III) recovers the line above, (a02)2+12∑n≥1(an2+bn2)\left(\frac{a_0}{2}\right)^2+\frac12\sum_{n\ge1}(a_n^2+b_n^2)(2a0​​)2+21​∑n≥1​(an2​+bn2​). The soul of the whole passage is one sentence: the basis is orthogonal, so the squared integral keeps only the "diagonal" terms.

Step 2: switching to the amplitude–phase coefficients cnc_ncn​​

Use the correspondence from the previous page: cn2=an2+bn2c_n^2=a_n^2+b_n^2cn2​=an2​+bn2​ (n≥1n\ge1n≥1), and the DC coefficient c0≡a0c_0\equiv a_0c0​≡a0​. Substituting into Step 1:

12π∫02π∣Γ∣2 dx=(c02)2+12∑n=1∞cn2=c024+12∑n=1∞cn2.\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|\Gamma|^2\,dx =\left(\frac{c_0}{2}\right)^2+\frac12\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n^2 =\frac{c_0^2}{4}+\frac12\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n^2.2π1​∫02π​∣Γ∣2dx=(2c0​​)2+21​n=1∑∞​cn2​=4c02​​+21​n=1∑∞​cn2​.
  • Simplification trick: note that c024=12⋅c022\dfrac{c_0^2}{4}=\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{c_0^2}{2}4c02​​=21​⋅2c02​​. To fold DC into the same summation, Hajimiri–Lee adopt the bookkeeping "c0c_0c0​ carries half weight inside the sum" — i.e. the n=0n=0n=0 term contributes 12⋅c022\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{c_0^2}{2}21​⋅2c02​​. The next step shows this is exactly what lets the total be written in its cleanest form.

Step 3: arriving at [P1] Eq.(20) — defining Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​​

Multiply both sides of Step 2 by 2:

1π∫02π∣Γ∣2 dx=c022+∑n=1∞cn2.\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|\Gamma|^2\,dx =\frac{c_0^2}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n^2.π1​∫02π​∣Γ∣2dx=2c02​​+n=1∑∞​cn2​.

In [P1] Eq.(20), Hajimiri–Lee define the left side as 2Γrms22\Gamma_{rms}^22Γrms2​ and write the right side as ∑n=0∞cn2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n^2∑n=0∞​cn2​ — i.e. by convention the n=0n=0n=0 term inside the sum stands for c022\dfrac{c_0^2}{2}2c02​​ (not c02c_0^2c02​). This yields the signature equation at the top of this page:

 ∑n=0∞cn2=1π∫02π∣Γ(x)∣2dx=2 Γrms2 [P1] Eq.(20), p.185\boxed{\ \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n^2=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|\Gamma(x)|^2dx=2\,\Gamma_{rms}^2\ }\qquad[\text{P1] Eq.(20), p.185} n=0∑∞​cn2​=π1​∫02π​∣Γ(x)∣2dx=2Γrms2​ ​[P1] Eq.(20), p.185

where Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​ is simply the root-mean-square of the ISF:

Γrms=12π∫02π∣Γ(x)∣2 dx.\Gamma_{rms}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|\Gamma(x)|^2\,dx}.Γrms​=2π1​∫02π​∣Γ(x)∣2dx​.
  • Dimension check: Γ\GammaΓ is dimensionless ⟹ ∣Γ∣2|\Gamma|^2∣Γ∣2 is dimensionless ⟹ dividing the integral by 2π2\pi2π (rad) and taking the square root leaves it dimensionless ✓. cnc_ncn​ is dimensionless too, so both sides agree.
  • This site's Python: gamma_rms(theta, gamma) computes exactly 12π∫02πΓ2\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\Gamma^2}2π1​∫02π​Γ2​ (see the simulations/common/isf_utils.py docstring, explicitly matched to Eq.(20)).

Step 4: teaching note on the DC-term factor (the most error-prone spot)​

Read this section carefully — otherwise you will be off by a factor when computing the 1/f31/f^31/f3 corner ([P1] Eq.(24)). Keep the three flavors of "c0c_0c0​" straight:

  • Why DC enters the sum at "half weight": an AC harmonic cncos⁡(nx+θn)c_n\cos(nx+\theta_n)cn​cos(nx+θn​) has mean square 12cn2\dfrac12 c_n^221​cn2​ (the time average of cosine, ⟨cos⁡2⟩=12\langle\cos^2\rangle=\frac12⟨cos2⟩=21​); but DC is a constant, whose mean square is (c02)2=c024\left(\dfrac{c_0}{2}\right)^2 =\dfrac{c_0^2}{4}(2c0​​)2=4c02​​.   • Teaching reminder: many textbooks (and the notation traps in this site's notation) hammer this point — c0c_0c0​ is the "coefficient"; the DC "value" is c0/2c_0/2c0​/2. The constants in the phase-noise formulas (e.g. c02/8c_0^2/8c02​/8 in Eq.(23), c02/(2Γrms2)c_0^2/(2\Gamma_{rms}^2)c02​/(2Γrms2​) in Eq.(24)) are exactly this factor carried all the way through. Copy the equations from Section 3 of the spec verbatim — do not re-insert factors yourself — and you will not go wrong.

Step 5: how Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​ sets the 1/f21/f^21/f2 phase noise​

Substitute Eq.(20) into the white-noise phase-noise summation ([P1] Eq.(19), p.185):

L{Δω}=10log⁡10 ⁣(in2‾/Δf  ∑n=0∞cn28 qmax2 Δω2).\mathcal{L}\{\Delta\omega\}=10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n^2}{8\,q_{max}^2\,\Delta\omega^2}\right).L{Δω}=10log10​(8qmax2​Δω2in2​​/Δf∑n=0∞​cn2​​).

Using ∑n=0∞cn2=2Γrms2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n^2=2\Gamma_{rms}^2∑n=0∞​cn2​=2Γrms2​ to replace ∑cn2\sum c_n^2∑cn2​, the 888 in the denominator and the 222 in the numerator reduce to 444, giving the signature result ([P1] Eq.(21), p.185):

L{Δω}=10log⁡10 ⁣(Γrms2qmax2⋅in2‾/Δf4 Δω2).\mathcal{L}\{\Delta\omega\}=10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\Gamma_{rms}^2}{q_{max}^2}\cdot\frac{\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f}{4\,\Delta\omega^2}\right).L{Δω}=10log10​(qmax2​Γrms2​​⋅4Δω2in2​​/Δf​).
  • How to read it: phase noise is proportional to Γrms2qmax2\dfrac{\Gamma_{rms}^2}{q_{max}^2}qmax2​Γrms2​​ and falls with offset Δω\Delta\omegaΔω as 1/Δω21/\Delta\omega^21/Δω2 (i.e. −20-20−20 dB/dec, the 1/f21/f^21/f2 region). Two design knobs: increase qmaxq_{max}qmax​ (the signal charge swing) and shrink Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​ (keep the ISF quiet overall).
  • Factor-of-2 teaching note: a clean time-domain "white noise × ISF → integrate" derivation gives Sϕ(f)=Γrms2Si/(qmax2(2πf)2)S_\phi(f)=\Gamma_{rms}^2 S_i/(q_{max}^2(2\pi f)^2)Sϕ​(f)=Γrms2​Si​/(qmax2​(2πf)2), corresponding to L=Γrms2Si/(2qmax2Δω2)\mathcal{L}=\Gamma_{rms}^2 S_i/(2q_{max}^2\Delta\omega^2)L=Γrms2​Si​/(2qmax2​Δω2); [P1] Eq.(21) writes /(4Δω2)/(4\Delta\omega^2)/(4Δω2). Full discussion in white_noise_to_phase_noise.

The ring's Γrms∝N−3/2\Gamma_{rms}\propto N^{-3/2}Γrms​∝N−3/2 scaling​

[P2] quantifies the observation above into a scaling law ([P2] Eq.(16), p.794):

Γrms∝N−3/2\Gamma_{rms}\propto N^{-3/2}Γrms​∝N−3/2

  • Intuition: as the stage count NNN increases, (i) each stage's transition becomes steeper and the ISF pulse narrower (rms drops), and (ii) the number of transitions per period grows but is diluted by the period length. Combined, this gives Γrms2∝N−3\Gamma_{rms}^2\propto N^{-3}Γrms2​∝N−3 (i.e. Γrms∝N−3/2\Gamma_{rms}\propto N^{-3/2}Γrms​∝N−3/2).

  • Full expression: [P2] Eq.(16) is

    Γrms=2π23η3  1N1.5,\Gamma_{rms}=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi^2}{3\eta^3}}\;\dfrac{1}{N^{1.5}},Γrms​=3η32π2​​N1.51​,

    where η\etaη is the stage-delay proportionality constant ([P2] Eq.(14), η≈1\eta\approx1η≈1 — not γ\gammaγ).   • Key conclusion: [P2] further shows that under the constraint of fixed f0f_0f0​ and power PPP, a single-ended ring's 1/f21/f^21/f2 phase noise/jitter is nearly independent of the stage count NNN ([P2] Sec.V, Eq.(23)/(25), p.796, L∣1/f2≈83η VDDVchar kTP(ω0/Δω)2\mathcal{L}\big|_{1/f^2}\approx\dfrac{8}{3\eta}\,\dfrac{V_{DD}}{V_{char}}\,\dfrac{kT}{P}(\omega_0/\Delta\omega)^2L​1/f2​≈3η8​Vchar​VDD​​PkT​(ω0​/Δω)2).

Worked example 1: Γrms=1/2\Gamma_{rms}=1/\sqrt2Γrms​=1/2​ for Γ=−sin⁡\Gamma=-\sinΓ=−sin​

Problem: for the ideal LC, Γ(θ)=−sin⁡θ\Gamma(\theta)=-\sin\thetaΓ(θ)=−sinθ. Find Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​.

Step-by-step substitution: apply the definition directly, Γrms2=12π∫02πΓ2 dθ\Gamma_{rms}^2=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\Gamma^2\,d\thetaΓrms2​=2π1​∫02π​Γ2dθ.

Γrms2=12π∫02π(−sin⁡θ)2 dθ=12π∫02πsin⁡2θ dθ=12π∫02π1−cos⁡2θ2 dθ=12π[θ2−sin⁡2θ4]02π=12π⋅2π2=12.\begin{aligned} \Gamma_{rms}^2 &=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}(-\sin\theta)^2\,d\theta =\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta\\ &=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{1-\cos2\theta}{2}\,d\theta =\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\frac{\theta}{2}-\frac{\sin2\theta}{4}\right]_0^{2\pi} =\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{2}=\frac12. \end{aligned}Γrms2​​=2π1​∫02π​(−sinθ)2dθ=2π1​∫02π​sin2θdθ=2π1​∫02π​21−cos2θ​dθ=2π1​[2θ​−4sin2θ​]02π​=2π1​⋅22π​=21​.​

Result: Γrms=1/2=12≈0.707\Gamma_{rms}=\sqrt{1/2}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\approx0.707Γrms​=1/2​=2​1​≈0.707. The second line uses the half-angle identity sin⁡2θ=12(1−cos⁡2θ)\sin^2\theta=\tfrac12(1-\cos2\theta)sin2θ=21​(1−cos2θ); cos⁡2θ\cos2\thetacos2θ integrates to 0 over a full period.

Dimension check: Γ=−sin⁡θ\Gamma=-\sin\thetaΓ=−sinθ is dimensionless → Γ2\Gamma^2Γ2 dimensionless → integrating over θ\thetaθ (rad), dividing by 2π2\pi2π (rad), and taking the square root leaves it dimensionless ✓.

import numpy as np

Worked example 2: Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​ of the triangular (ring-toy) ISF​

Problem: the ring's pedagogical toy ISF uses this site's gamma_triangular (N=5N=5N=5): a triangle wave with peak P=1/N=1/5P=1/\sqrt N=1/\sqrt5P=1/N​=1/5​, ramping linearly back and forth between [−P,P][-P,P][−P,P] (two transitions per period). Find Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​. (Toy model, not transistor-level.)

Step-by-step substitution: the triangle wave is linear on each basic ramp, so the mean square follows the standard result "mean square of a linear segment over [−P,P][-P,P][−P,P] =P2/3=P^2/3=P2/3". Reason: write the linear segment as Γ=P s\Gamma=P\,sΓ=Ps (with sss sweeping uniformly from −1-1−1 to 111),

⟨Γ2⟩=12∫−11(Ps)2 ds=P22⋅s33∣−11=P22⋅23=P23.\langle\Gamma^2\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}(P s)^2\,ds=\frac{P^2}{2}\cdot\frac{s^3}{3}\Big|_{-1}^{1}=\frac{P^2}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{3}=\frac{P^2}{3}.⟨Γ2⟩=21​∫−11​(Ps)2ds=2P2​⋅3s3​​−11​=2P2​⋅32​=3P2​.

Substituting P=1/5P=1/\sqrt5P=1/5​:

Γrms2=P23=1/53=115≈0.0667 ⇒ Γrms=115≈0.258.\Gamma_{rms}^2=\frac{P^2}{3}=\frac{1/5}{3}=\frac{1}{15}\approx0.0667 \ \Rightarrow\ \Gamma_{rms}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{15}}\approx0.258.Γrms2​=3P2​=31/5​=151​≈0.0667 ⇒ Γrms​=15​1​≈0.258.

Result: Γrms≈0.258\Gamma_{rms}\approx0.258Γrms​≈0.258, far below the LC's 0.707 — consistent with the physics that "the ring squeezes its sensitivity into narrow transitions, the energy is flattened out, and Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​ shrinks with NNN" (echoing the Γrms∝N−3/2\Gamma_{rms}\propto N^{-3/2}Γrms​∝N−3/2 trend; the toy's NNN dependence here is 1/N1/\sqrt N1/N​, not the real scaling).

Dimension check: PPP is dimensionless (the ISF is dimensionless) → Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​ dimensionless ✓.

Worked example 3: numerically verifying ∑cn2=2Γrms2\sum c_n^2=2\Gamma_{rms}^2∑cn2​=2Γrms2​ (Parseval reconciliation)​

Problem: for the triangular ISF of worked example 2 (N=5N=5N=5), compute ∑n=0∞cn2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n^2∑n=0∞​cn2​ from the coefficient side and 2Γrms22\Gamma_{rms}^22Γrms2​ from the integral side, and verify that the two are equal.

Step-by-step substitution: from worked example 2, 2Γrms2=2×115=215≈0.13332\Gamma_{rms}^2=2\times\dfrac{1}{15}=\dfrac{2}{15}\approx0.13332Γrms2​=2×151​=152​≈0.1333. On the coefficient side, add up the squared cnc_ncn​, remembering the half weight c02/2c_0^2/2c02​/2 for n=0n=0n=0 (the triangle wave is symmetric, c0=0c_0=0c0​=0, so DC contributes nothing). Parseval guarantees the two numbers are equal.

Result: ∑n=0∞cn2=2Γrms2≈0.1333\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n^2=2\Gamma_{rms}^2\approx0.1333∑n=0∞​cn2​=2Γrms2​≈0.1333 ✓.

Dimension check: cnc_ncn​ and Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​ are all dimensionless; both sides agree ✓.

import numpy as np
from simulations.common.isf_utils import (
    gamma_triangular, compute_fourier_coefficients, gamma_rms,
)
theta = np.linspace(0.0, 2*np.pi, 4096, endpoint=True)
gamma = gamma_triangular(theta, n_stages=5)
a0, a, b, c, ph = compute_fourier_coefficients(theta, gamma, n_harmonics=32)
lhs = 0.5*c[0]**2 + np.sum(c[1:]**2)   # half weight for n=0
rhs = 2.0*gamma_rms(theta, gamma)**2
print(lhs, rhs)                        # -> ~0.1333 , 0.1333  (more harmonics, closer match)
  • Feel: a triangle wave carries "infinitely many odd harmonics", so the coefficient side needs enough n_harmonics (e.g. 32) to approach 2Γrms22\Gamma_{rms}^22Γrms2​; taking too few harmonics (e.g. 8) slightly undershoots the integral value — which is itself a numerical portrait of "the ring ISF's energy spread across high harmonics".

Key takeaways​

  • Parseval: ∑n=0∞cn2=1π∫02π∣Γ∣2dx=2Γrms2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n^2=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|\Gamma|^2dx=2\Gamma_{rms}^2∑n=0∞​cn2​=π1​∫02π​∣Γ∣2dx=2Γrms2​ ([P1] Eq.(20), p.185).
  • Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​ collapses the "band-by-band fold-back" into one shape metric; together with qmaxq_{max}qmax​ it sets the 1/f21/f^21/f2 phase noise: L∝Γrms2/qmax2\mathcal{L}\propto\Gamma_{rms}^2/q_{max}^2L∝Γrms2​/qmax2​ ([P1] Eq.(21)).
  • DC-factor convention: c0c_0c0​ is the coefficient; the DC value is c0/2c_0/2c0​/2; the n=0n=0n=0 term in the Parseval sum contributes c02/2c_0^2/2c02​/2 (not c02c_0^2c02​). Just copy the spec's equations.
  • Ideal LC: Γrms=1/2≈0.707\Gamma_{rms}=1/\sqrt2\approx0.707Γrms​=1/2​≈0.707; canonical Example B with Γrms=0.5\Gamma_{rms}=0.5Γrms​=0.5 gives L(1MHz)≈−148\mathcal{L}(1\text{MHz})\approx-148L(1MHz)≈−148 dBc/Hz.
  • Ring: Γrms∝N−3/2\Gamma_{rms}\propto N^{-3/2}Γrms​∝N−3/2 ([P2] Eq.(16), p.794; the radical covers only the constant, ≈4/N1.5\approx4/N^{1.5}≈4/N1.5 at η=0.75\eta=0.75η=0.75, corroborated three ways by the main text and App.B; a v3 misreading of the radical's scope had this as N−3/4N^{-3/4}N−3/4, corrected back in v7).
