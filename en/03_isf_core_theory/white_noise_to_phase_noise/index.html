How White Noise Becomes 1/f² Phase Noise

β: This English translation is in beta — the Traditional-Chinese original is the authoritative version.

Prerequisites: convolution_derivation | Next: fourier_series_of_isf · rms_isf

Hands-on verification: see lab_06 for this page's "white noise → 1/f21/f^21/f2 phase noise" time-domain simulation matching theory.

This page answers the most iconic result in oscillator phase-noise theory: why does a spectrally flat white current noise (a random current whose power spectral density is independent of frequency), after passing through an oscillator, turn into a phase-noise skirt with a slope of −20-20−20 dB/decade (i.e., 1/f21/f^21/f2)? We will derive all the way to

L{Δω}=10log⁡10 ⁣(Γrms2qmax2⋅in2‾/Δf4 Δω2)\mathcal{L}\{\Delta\omega\}=10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\Gamma_{rms}^2}{q_{max}^2}\cdot\frac{\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f}{4\,\Delta\omega^2}\right)L{Δω}=10log10​(qmax2​Γrms2​​⋅4Δω2in2​​/Δf​)

and honestly account for the famous "off by a factor of 2" bookkeeping issue in the literature.

Physical intuition (conclusion first): white noise is itself "flat" — it has no 1/f1/f1/f structure whatsoever. But the previous chapter convolution_derivation told us: the phase ϕ(t)\phi(t)ϕ(t) is the time integral of the noise current ([P1] Eq.(11)). An integrator is a 1/(jω)1/(j\omega)1/(jω) filter: it multiplies the power spectrum by 1/ω21/\omega^21/ω2. Something flat goes in, gets multiplied by 1/ω21/\omega^21/ω2, and comes out — that is where 1/f21/f^21/f2 comes from. The ISF only decides "how large a weight to multiply by" (set by Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​ and qmaxq_{max}qmax​); it does not decide the slope. The slope always comes from the integrator.

Recap first: phase is the integral of noise​

Start from the LTV phase response of [P1] Eq.(11), p.182:

ϕ(t)=1qmax∫−∞tΓ(ω0τ) in(τ) dτ.\phi(t)=\frac{1}{q_{max}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\Gamma(\omega_0\tau)\,i_n(\tau)\,d\tau .ϕ(t)=qmax​1​∫−∞t​Γ(ω0​τ)in​(τ)dτ.

Read it as a signal flow: the noise current in(t)i_n(t)in​(t) is first modulated (pointwise multiplication) by the periodic weight Γ(ω0t)/qmax\Gamma(\omega_0 t)/q_{max}Γ(ω0​t)/qmax​, and then integrated (with memory up to the upper limit ttt). As a block diagram:

The whole task is: trace the power spectrum through this chain — the input is SiS_iSi​; what is the output SϕS_\phiSϕ​?

Step 1: what is the PSD of white current noise​

White noise is by definition noise whose PSD is independent of frequency (flat). Using the single-sided PSD, we write

Si(f)=in2‾Δf[A2/Hz].S_i(f)=\frac{\overline{i_n^2}}{\Delta f}\quad[\text{A}^2/\text{Hz}].Si​(f)=Δfin2​​​[A2/Hz].
  • Physics used: thermal noise and shot noise can both be treated as white within the offset band we care about (kHz–MHz) — their corner frequencies lie far higher.
  • Unit check: [A2/Hz][\text{A}^2/\text{Hz}][A2/Hz]; multiplying by a bandwidth Δf\Delta fΔf (Hz) gives [A2][\text{A}^2][A2] = mean-square current ✓.
  • Order-of-magnitude feel: the thermal noise current of a conducting resistance RRR is in2‾/Δf=4kT/R\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f=4kT/Rin2​​/Δf=4kT/R; at R=1 kΩR=1\,\text{k}\OmegaR=1kΩ and room temperature this is about 1.6×10−23 A2/Hz1.6\times10^{-23}\,\text{A}^2/\text{Hz}1.6×10−23A2/Hz. The canonical Example B on this page takes Si=10−24 A2/HzS_i=10^{-24}\,\text{A}^2/\text{Hz}Si​=10−24A2/Hz as a round-number "single equivalent white-noise source", convenient for mental arithmetic.

Step 2: ISF modulation — white noise "stirred" by the periodic weight​

Expand Γ(ω0τ)\Gamma(\omega_0\tau)Γ(ω0​τ) in a Fourier series ([P1] Eq.(12), p.183):

Γ(ω0τ)=c02+∑n=1∞cncos⁡(nω0τ+θn).\Gamma(\omega_0\tau)=\frac{c_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n\cos(n\omega_0\tau+\theta_n).Γ(ω0​τ)=2c0​​+n=1∑∞​cn​cos(nω0​τ+θn​).

Multiplying by the white noise in(τ)i_n(\tau)in​(τ) amounts to picking up the noise near each harmonic nω0n\omega_0nω0​ and "down-converting" it back to baseband (this is the frequency translation described in fourier_series_of_isf). The key point: for white noise, the noise power near every harmonic nω0n\omega_0nω0​ is equally large (because the spectrum is flat), so every cnc_ncn​ brings down white noise of the same strength. The harmonics are uncorrelated (white noise in different frequency bands is mutually independent), so powers add directly, and the total weight is ∑ncn2\sum_n c_n^2∑n​cn2​.

  • Math used: white noise in different bands is uncorrelated → the harmonic contributions add in power (not in amplitude).
  • Unit check: cnc_ncn​ is dimensionless, so ∑cn2\sum c_n^2∑cn2​ is dimensionless ✓.

Step 3: from single-tone sidebands to the white-noise summation (Eq.(19))​

[P1]'s strategy is clever: first compute how much sideband "one small single-tone current" produces, then treat white noise as a superposition of countless independent small tones. For a tone injected near nω0n\omega_0nω0​, i(t)=I0cos⁡(nω0+Δω)ti(t)=I_0\cos(n\omega_0+\Delta\omega)ti(t)=I0​cos(nω0​+Δω)t, the excess phase is a slow modulation ([P1] Eq.(16/17), p.183):

ϕ(t)≈I0 cnsin⁡(Δωt)2qmax Δω.\phi(t)\approx\frac{I_0\,c_n\sin(\Delta\omega t)}{2q_{max}\,\Delta\omega}.ϕ(t)≈2qmax​ΔωI0​cn​sin(Δωt)​.

The single-sideband power it produces relative to the carrier ([P1] Eq.(18), p.183):

PSBC(Δω)=10log⁡10 ⁣(I0 cn4qmax Δω)2.P_{SBC}(\Delta\omega)=10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{I_0\,c_n}{4q_{max}\,\Delta\omega}\right)^2.PSBC​(Δω)=10log10​(4qmax​ΔωI0​cn​​)2.

Step 3a (worked out explicitly): the downconversion integral — the slow term survives, the fast term is averaged away​

Eq.(16/17) above does not appear out of thin air; it is the algebraic result of "down-converting the noise near nω0n\omega_0nω0​ to baseband". We now work it out for you step by step — this is exactly the intermediate step that the authoring spec (§10.2) requires.

Substitute the near-nω0n\omega_0nω0​ injected tone i(τ)=I0cos⁡((nω0+Δω)τ)i(\tau)=I_0\cos((n\omega_0+\Delta\omega)\tau)i(τ)=I0​cos((nω0​+Δω)τ) into the nnn-th harmonic term of [P1] Eq.(13). The integral to evaluate for that term is "the ISF's nnn-th harmonic weight" times "the injected current", integrated:

ϕn(t)=1qmax∫t ⁣ ⁣cncos⁡(nω0τ+θn) I0cos⁡((nω0+Δω)τ) dτ.\phi_n(t)=\frac{1}{q_{max}}\int^{t}\!\!c_n\cos(n\omega_0\tau+\theta_n)\,I_0\cos\big((n\omega_0+\Delta\omega)\tau\big)\,d\tau .ϕn​(t)=qmax​1​∫tcn​cos(nω0​τ+θn​)I0​cos((nω0​+Δω)τ)dτ.

Step (i): use the product-to-sum identity to split the product of the two cosines into a "sum" cosine and a "difference" cosine. The identity

cos⁡Acos⁡B=12[cos⁡(A−B)+cos⁡(A+B)]\cos A\cos B=\tfrac12\big[\cos(A-B)+\cos(A+B)\big]cosAcosB=21​[cos(A−B)+cos(A+B)]

Let A=nω0τ+θnA=n\omega_0\tau+\theta_nA=nω0​τ+θn​ and B=(nω0+Δω)τB=(n\omega_0+\Delta\omega)\tauB=(nω0​+Δω)τ. Compute the difference and sum frequencies:

A−B=(nω0τ+θn)−(nω0+Δω)τ=θn−Δωτ,A-B=(n\omega_0\tau+\theta_n)-(n\omega_0+\Delta\omega)\tau=\theta_n-\Delta\omega\tau,A−B=(nω0​τ+θn​)−(nω0​+Δω)τ=θn​−Δωτ, A+B=(nω0τ+θn)+(nω0+Δω)τ=(2nω0+Δω)τ+θn.A+B=(n\omega_0\tau+\theta_n)+(n\omega_0+\Delta\omega)\tau=(2n\omega_0+\Delta\omega)\tau+\theta_n .A+B=(nω0​τ+θn​)+(nω0​+Δω)τ=(2nω0​+Δω)τ+θn​.

So the integrand becomes

cncos⁡(nω0τ+θn) I0cos⁡((nω0+Δω)τ)=I0cn2[cos⁡(Δωτ−θn)⏟slow term, ≈Δω+cos⁡((2nω0+Δω)τ+θn)⏟fast term, ≈2nω0].c_n\cos(n\omega_0\tau+\theta_n)\,I_0\cos\big((n\omega_0+\Delta\omega)\tau\big)=\frac{I_0c_n}{2}\Big[\underbrace{\cos(\Delta\omega\tau-\theta_n)}_{\text{slow term, }\approx\Delta\omega}+\underbrace{\cos\big((2n\omega_0+\Delta\omega)\tau+\theta_n\big)}_{\text{fast term, }\approx 2n\omega_0}\Big].cn​cos(nω0​τ+θn​)I0​cos((nω0​+Δω)τ)=2I0​cn​​[slow term, ≈Δωcos(Δωτ−θn​)​​+fast term, ≈2nω0​cos((2nω0​+Δω)τ+θn​)​​].

(Using cos⁡(θn−Δωτ)=cos⁡(Δωτ−θn)\cos(\theta_n-\Delta\omega\tau)=\cos(\Delta\omega\tau-\theta_n)cos(θn​−Δωτ)=cos(Δωτ−θn​) — cosine is an even function.)

Step (ii): the two terms meet completely different fates. The integrator (that ∫tdτ\int^t d\tau∫tdτ) is essentially a low-pass — it accumulates the signal over time; high-frequency components "cancel between positive and negative excursions and get averaged away", while low-frequency (slow) components keep accumulating.

  • The slow term has frequency about Δω\Delta\omegaΔω (the offset, typically kHz–MHz, Δω≪ω0\Delta\omega\ll\omega_0Δω≪ω0​). Integrating a slow cosine gives ∫cos⁡(Δωτ−θn) dτ=sin⁡(Δωτ−θn)Δω\int\cos(\Delta\omega\tau-\theta_n)\,d\tau=\dfrac{\sin(\Delta\omega\tau-\theta_n)}{\Delta\omega}∫cos(Δωτ−θn​)dτ=Δωsin(Δωτ−θn​)​ — the denominator is only a tiny Δω\Delta\omegaΔω, so the term is amplified and survives.
  • The fast term has frequency about 2nω02n\omega_02nω0​ (twice-the-carrier scale, GHz). The same integration gives sin⁡(⋯ )2nω0+Δω\dfrac{\sin(\cdots)}{2n\omega_0+\Delta\omega}2nω0​+Δωsin(⋯)​ — the denominator is the huge 2nω02n\omega_02nω0​, squeezing the amplitude down to a factor of ∼Δω/(2nω0)\sim\Delta\omega/(2n\omega_0)∼Δω/(2nω0​); negligible relative to the slow term, it is effectively averaged away by the integrator.

Step (iii): keep only the slow term, obtaining Eq.(16/17).

ϕn(t)≈1qmax⋅I0cn2⋅sin⁡(Δωt−θn)Δω=I0 cnsin⁡(Δωt−θn)2qmax Δω\phi_n(t)\approx\frac{1}{q_{max}}\cdot\frac{I_0c_n}{2}\cdot\frac{\sin(\Delta\omega t-\theta_n)}{\Delta\omega}=\frac{I_0\,c_n\sin(\Delta\omega t-\theta_n)}{2q_{max}\,\Delta\omega}ϕn​(t)≈qmax​1​⋅2I0​cn​​⋅Δωsin(Δωt−θn​)​=2qmax​ΔωI0​cn​sin(Δωt−θn​)​

Absorbing the time-independent phase θn\theta_nθn​ into the time origin recovers [P1] Eq.(16/17) above, ϕ(t)≈I0cnsin⁡(Δωt)2qmaxΔω\phi(t)\approx\dfrac{I_0 c_n\sin(\Delta\omega t)}{2q_{max}\Delta\omega}ϕ(t)≈2qmax​ΔωI0​cn​sin(Δωt)​. This downconversion integral (spec §10.2) is the hard core of "the oscillator acting as a mixer": noise near nω0n\omega_0nω0​ is moved by the ISF's nnn-th harmonic to Δω\Delta\omegaΔω (the difference-frequency slow term survives), while the sum-frequency fast term at 2nω02n\omega_02nω0​ vanishes automatically.

  • Unit check: [A]⋅(dimensionless)[C]⋅[rad/s]=AA⋅s⋅s⋅rad−1\dfrac{[\text{A}]\cdot(\text{dimensionless})}{[\text{C}]\cdot[\text{rad/s}]}=\dfrac{\text{A}}{\text{A}\cdot\text{s}}\cdot\text{s}\cdot\text{rad}^{-1}[C]⋅[rad/s][A]⋅(dimensionless)​=A⋅sA​⋅s⋅rad−1 — with C=A⋅sC=\text{A}\cdot\text{s}C=A⋅s and Δω\Delta\omegaΔω in rad/s, this simplifies so that ϕ\phiϕ is dimensionless (rad) ✓.

Step 3b (worked out explicitly): the factor-8 summation, (18)→(19)​

Next, accumulate the "single-tone sideband powers" into the "white-noise summation". Three things hide in this step: both sidebands, the white-noise substitution I02/2→in2‾/ΔfI_0^2/2\to\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta fI02​/2→in2​​/Δf, and the sum over nnn. Unpacked step by step:

Step (i): the linear value of the single-sideband relative power. Square the quantity inside Eq.(18)'s parentheses (hold off on the 10log⁡10\log10log):

(I0 cn4qmax Δω)2=I02 cn216 qmax2 Δω2.\left(\frac{I_0\,c_n}{4q_{max}\,\Delta\omega}\right)^2=\frac{I_0^2\,c_n^2}{16\,q_{max}^2\,\Delta\omega^2}.(4qmax​ΔωI0​cn​​)2=16qmax2​Δω2I02​cn2​​.

(The 444 here comes from the 2qmaxΔω2q_{max}\Delta\omega2qmax​Δω of Eq.(16) plus taking a single sideband — the power of the slow modulation sin⁡(Δωt)\sin(\Delta\omega t)sin(Δωt) corresponds to 12\tfrac1221​ of the amplitude, 12×12=14\tfrac12\times\tfrac1{2}=\tfrac1421​×21​=41​, so the denominator goes from 222 to 444; hence 42=164^2=1642=16.)

Step (ii): the white-noise substitution I02/2→in2‾/ΔfI_0^2/2\to\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta fI02​/2→in2​​/Δf. A single tone of amplitude I0I_0I0​ has mean square (power) I02/2I_0^2/2I02​/2 (sinusoid rms² === amplitude²/2/2/2). View the white noise as "one equivalent small tone near each nω0n\omega_0nω0​, within bandwidth Δf\Delta fΔf"; its power is the PSD times the bandwidth =(in2‾/Δf)=(\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f)=(in2​​/Δf). I022  →  in2‾Δf⟹I02  →  2 in2‾Δf.\frac{I_0^2}{2}\;\to\;\frac{\overline{i_n^2}}{\Delta f}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad I_0^2\;\to\;2\,\frac{\overline{i_n^2}}{\Delta f}.2I02​​→Δfin2​​​⟹I02​→2Δfin2​​​.

I02 cn216 qmax2 Δω2  →  2(in2‾/Δf) cn216 qmax2 Δω2.\frac{I_0^2\,c_n^2}{16\,q_{max}^2\,\Delta\omega^2}\;\to\;\frac{2(\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f)\,c_n^2}{16\,q_{max}^2\,\Delta\omega^2}.16qmax2​Δω2I02​cn2​​→16qmax2​Δω22(in2​​/Δf)cn2​​.

Step (iii): both sidebands + sum over nnn. Each harmonic nω0n\omega_0nω0​ folds one sideband back from each side of the carrier (both nω0+Δωn\omega_0+\Delta\omeganω0​+Δω and nω0−Δωn\omega_0-\Delta\omeganω0​−Δω down-convert to Δω\Delta\omegaΔω); the power above already contains both contributions. L=∑n2(in2‾/Δf) cn216 qmax2 Δω2=(in2‾/Δf)∑ncn28 qmax2 Δω2.\mathcal{L}=\sum_n\frac{2(\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f)\,c_n^2}{16\,q_{max}^2\,\Delta\omega^2}=\frac{(\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f)\sum_n c_n^2}{8\,q_{max}^2\,\Delta\omega^2}.L=n∑​16qmax2​Δω22(in2​​/Δf)cn2​​=8qmax2​Δω2(in2​​/Δf)∑n​cn2​​.

Step (iv): cancel to get the factor 8. Numerator 222, denominator 161616; cancelling leaves 888 — this is the origin of that famous 888 in Eq.(19)'s denominator:

Treating the white noise as countless independent tones near each nω0n\omega_0nω0​ within bandwidth Δf\Delta fΔf, letting I02/2→in2‾/ΔfI_0^2/2\to\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta fI02​/2→in2​​/Δf, and summing over all harmonics n=0,1,2,…n=0,1,2,\dotsn=0,1,2,… gives the white-noise phase-noise summation ([P1] Eq.(19), p.185):

L{Δω}=10log⁡10 ⁣(in2‾/Δf  ∑n=0∞cn28 qmax2 Δω2)\mathcal{L}\{\Delta\omega\}=10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n^2}{8\,q_{max}^2\,\Delta\omega^2}\right)L{Δω}=10log10​(8qmax2​Δω2in2​​/Δf∑n=0∞​cn2​​)
  • See the 1/f21/f^21/f2 yet? The denominator carries Δω2\Delta\omega^2Δω2. Step 4: use Parseval to replace the sum by Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​ (Eq.(20))​

∑cn2\sum c_n^2∑cn2​ is hard to measure directly, but it equals the "total energy" of the ISF. The Parseval relation ([P1] Eq.(20), p.185):

∑n=0∞cn2=1π∫02π∣Γ(x)∣2dx=2 Γrms2.\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n^2=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|\Gamma(x)|^2dx=2\,\Gamma_{rms}^2.n=0∑∞​cn2​=π1​∫02π​∣Γ(x)∣2dx=2Γrms2​.
  • Math used: the sum of squared Fourier coefficients = the mean square of the function (Parseval/Rayleigh); see rms_isf for details. Note the right-hand side is 2Γrms22\Gamma_{rms}^22Γrms2​ (not Γrms2\Gamma_{rms}^2Γrms2​), because Γrms2=12π∫02π∣Γ∣2dx\Gamma_{rms}^2=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|\Gamma|^2dxΓrms2​=2π1​∫02π​∣Γ∣2dx — a factor of 222 apart.
  • Physical meaning: you do not need to know every individual cnc_ncn​; knowing the ISF's rms value alone suffices to compute the white-noise phase noise.

Step 5: substitute to get the signature 1/f² result (Eq.(21))​

Substitute ∑cn2=2Γrms2\sum c_n^2=2\Gamma_{rms}^2∑cn2​=2Γrms2​ back into Eq.(19); the 888 in the denominator cancels by half, becoming 444:

in2‾/Δf⋅2Γrms28 qmax2 Δω2=Γrms2qmax2⋅in2‾/Δf4 Δω2.\frac{\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f\cdot 2\Gamma_{rms}^2}{8\,q_{max}^2\,\Delta\omega^2}=\frac{\Gamma_{rms}^2}{q_{max}^2}\cdot\frac{\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f}{4\,\Delta\omega^2}.8qmax2​Δω2in2​​/Δf⋅2Γrms2​​=qmax2​Γrms2​​⋅4Δω2in2​​/Δf​.

This yields the white-noise 1/f² result ([P1] Eq.(21), p.185):

 L{Δω}=10log⁡10 ⁣(Γrms2qmax2⋅in2‾/Δf4 Δω2) \boxed{\ \mathcal{L}\{\Delta\omega\}=10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\Gamma_{rms}^2}{q_{max}^2}\cdot\frac{\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta f}{4\,\Delta\omega^2}\right)\ } L{Δω}=10log10​(qmax2​Γrms2​​⋅4Δω2in2​​/Δf​) ​
  • Design message (claim C3): phase noise is proportional to Γrms2/qmax2\Gamma_{rms}^2/q_{max}^2Γrms2​/qmax2​. To lower the 1/f21/f^21/f2 noise, increase the signal charge swing qmaxq_{max}qmax​ (squared in the denominator — a very strong lever) and reduce the ISF's rms value Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​. The relation between Sϕ(f)S_\phi(f)Sϕ​(f) and L(Δf)\mathcal{L}(\Delta f)L(Δf) (dBc/Hz intuition)​

Engineering practice commonly uses two quantities to describe the same thing; you must be able to convert between them:

  • phase PSD Sϕ(f)S_\phi(f)Sϕ​(f): the power spectral density of phase fluctuations, in rad2/Hz\text{rad}^2/\text{Hz}rad2/Hz; integrating over fff gives the phase variance σϕ2\sigma_\phi^2σϕ2​.
  • SSB phase noise L(Δf)\mathcal{L}(\Delta f)L(Δf): single-sideband noise power per Hz relative to the carrier, in dBc/Hz.

Under the small-angle approximation (phase fluctuations far below 1 rad) the two are related by (spec Eq. 16):

L(Δf)≈12Sϕ(Δf).\mathcal{L}(\Delta f)\approx\tfrac12 S_\phi(\Delta f).L(Δf)≈21​Sϕ​(Δf).

Writing the white-noise result in phase-PSD form (clean time-domain derivation, see the section below):

Sϕ(f)=Γrms2qmax2⋅Si(2πf)2[rad2/Hz].S_\phi(f)=\frac{\Gamma_{rms}^2}{q_{max}^2}\cdot\frac{S_i}{(2\pi f)^2}\quad[\text{rad}^2/\text{Hz}].Sϕ​(f)=qmax2​Γrms2​​⋅(2πf)2Si​​[rad2/Hz].
  • dBc/Hz intuition: −100-100−100 dBc/Hz means "at that offset from the carrier, within a 1 Hz bandwidth, the noise power is 101010^{10}1010 times smaller than the carrier". The more negative the number, the cleaner. In the 1/f21/f^21/f2 region, every decade further out improves the dBc/Hz figure by 20 (20 more negative).

The important factor-of-2 teaching note (make sure you understand it)​

If you do the clean time-domain derivation yourself (white noise ×\times× ISF →\to→ integration), you get

Sϕ(f)=Γrms2 Siqmax2(2πf)2,⇒L(Δf)≈12Sϕ=Γrms2qmax2⋅Si2 Δω2.S_\phi(f)=\frac{\Gamma_{rms}^2\,S_i}{q_{max}^2(2\pi f)^2},\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\mathcal{L}(\Delta f)\approx\tfrac12 S_\phi=\frac{\Gamma_{rms}^2}{q_{max}^2}\cdot\frac{S_i}{2\,\Delta\omega^2}.Sϕ​(f)=qmax2​(2πf)2Γrms2​Si​​,⇒L(Δf)≈21​Sϕ​=qmax2​Γrms2​​⋅2Δω2Si​​.

That is, the denominator is 2 Δω22\,\Delta\omega^22Δω2. But [P1] Eq.(21) has 4 Δω24\,\Delta\omega^24Δω2. They differ by a factor of 2.

  • The gap comes from the SSB (single-sideband) bookkeeping convention: whether all of the phase-fluctuation power is charged to "a single sideband" or "split evenly across both sidebands", and whether in2‾/Δf\overline{i_n^2}/\Delta fin2​​/Δf is a single-sided or two-sided PSD — any inconsistency in definitions produces a factor of 2.
  • This is a famous minor controversy in the literature; the constant lands somewhere between /2/2/2 and /4/4/4 across textbooks/papers.
  • The key point: it does not affect the Γrms2/qmax2\Gamma_{rms}^2/q_{max}^2Γrms2​/qmax2​ scaling at all, nor the −20-20−20 dB/decade slope. Design work looks at scaling and slope; that ±3\pm3±3 dB constant is naturally absorbed during measurement calibration.
  • This site's lab_06 numerical simulation uses the clean time-domain version Sϕ=Γrms2Si/(qmax2(2πf)2)S_\phi=\Gamma_{rms}^2 S_i/(q_{max}^2(2\pi f)^2)Sϕ​=Γrms2​Si​/(qmax2​(2πf)2), so it differs from Eq.(21) by this factor of 2 — expected and taught, not a bug.

One sentence to remember: slope and scaling are physics; the constant 2 is bookkeeping. Do not lose sleep over that 2.

Key takeaways​

  • White noise is flat; the 1/f21/f^21/f2 slope comes entirely from the phase integrator's 1/ω21/\omega^21/ω2 — the ISF only sets the size of the weight.
  • Derivation chain: Eq.(19) summation → Eq.(20) Parseval (∑cn2=2Γrms2\sum c_n^2=2\Gamma_{rms}^2∑cn2​=2Γrms2​) → Eq.(21) signature result.
  • L∝Γrms2/qmax2⋅Si/Δω2\mathcal{L}\propto\Gamma_{rms}^2/q_{max}^2\cdot S_i/\Delta\omega^2L∝Γrms2​/qmax2​⋅Si​/Δω2; in design, increase qmaxq_{max}qmax​ and reduce Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​.
  • L≈12Sϕ\mathcal{L}\approx\frac12 S_\phiL≈21​Sϕ​; the 1/f21/f^21/f2 region improves by 20 dB per decade.
  • Factor-of-2: the clean time-domain version has /(2Δω2)/(2\Delta\omega^2)/(2Δω2); [P1] Eq.(21) has /(4Δω2)/(4\Delta\omega^2)/(4Δω2); the factor 2 comes from SSB bookkeeping and does not affect scaling/slope.
  • Canonical Example B: a single white-noise source gives ≈−148\approx-148≈−148 dBc/Hz @ 1 MHz (time-domain version −145-145−145, 3 dB apart).
