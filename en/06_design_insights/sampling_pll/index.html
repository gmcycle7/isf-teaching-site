Sampling / sub-sampling PLL — kicking the divider out of the loop

Prerequisites: pll_noise_budget (the five-source budget, where the in-band floor SrefN2+ScpS_{ref}N^2+S_{cp}Sref​N2+Scp​ comes from), clock_chain_budget (the +20log⁡10N+20\log_{10}N+20log10​N bookkeeping for ×N, ϕout=Nϕin\phi_{out}=N\phi_{in}ϕout​=Nϕin​), adc_aperture_jitter (aperture math: sampling error = slope × timing error) | Next: serdes_clocking_connection, exercises

The conclusion of pll_noise_budget was: for the classic charge-pump PLL, the in-band floor is set jointly by reference×N2\times N^2×N2 and the PFD/charge-pump/divider — a cleaner VCO doesn't help at all. This page asks the next question: is the loop's front end (PFD, charge-pump, divider) a physical limit, or an architectural choice? The answer is the latter — the sub-sampling PLL (an architecture that samples the high-speed VCO sinusoid directly with a low-speed reference) removes the divider from the phase-detection path entirely, boosting the phase-detector gain (KPDK_{PD}KPD​) from "Icp/2πI_{cp}/2\piIcp​/2π divided further by NNN" to "gm⋅Ag_m\cdot Agm​⋅A." As a result, the divider noise term vanishes, and CP noise is no longer amplified by ×N2\times N^2×N2 — the in-band floor drops until only the reference term remains. This is one of the most important architectural breakthroughs in PLL design over the last fifteen years.

External-literature note: the sub-sampling PLL architecture and the standard result "divider noise eliminated, PD/CP noise not multiplied by N2N^2N2" are not among the five PDFs downloaded for this site (external literature, not among the five source PDFs). Classic reference: X. Gao, E. A. M. Klumperink, M. Bohsali, and B. Nauta, "A Low Noise Sub-Sampling PLL in Which Divider Noise Is Eliminated and PD/CP Noise Is Not Multiplied by N²," IEEE J. Solid-State Circuits, vol. 44, no. 12, pp. 3253–3263, Dec. 2009. This page's derivation is self-contained, and every numeric value is labeled "illustrative"; the five source PDFs supply the other half of the physics — "sensitivity at the sampling point" ([P1]'s ISF).

Intuition first (the punchline up front): in the classic PLL, phase comparison means "divide 5 GHz down to 100 MHz, then compare phase" — dividing shrinks the phase by a factor of NNN (the signal weakens by NNN), while PFD/CP noise enters unscaled, so the SNR takes an inherent N×N\timesN× hit; the divider then tacks on its own noise on top. Sub-sampling flips this: "don't touch the VCO — sample the 5 GHz sinusoid directly with the 100 MHz reference edge." At lock, the sampling instant lands on the sinusoid's zero crossing — where the slope is steepest (Aω0A\omega_0Aω0​, V/s) — so a tiny VCO phase shift produces a large change in the sampled voltage. The phase-detector gain is "volt-scale" (AAA, V/rad), several orders of magnitude larger than the charge-pump's "microamp-scale" gain. The same electronic noise (in A/√Hz or V/√Hz) divided by a much larger KPDK_{PD}KPD​ converts to phase noise that is orders of magnitude smaller. The price: the sampler hangs directly off the VCO (reference spur, kT/C folding), and every zero crossing of the sinusoid looks identical (can't lock in NNN without an auxiliary loop).

Step 2: the sub-sampling idea — sample the VCO sinusoid directly with the reference edge​

The sub-sampling phase detector (SSPD) is simply a track-and-hold: every rising edge of the reference samples the VCO's sinusoidal voltage onto a capacitor CsC_sCs​. "Sub-sampling" refers to the sampling rate fref≪f0f_{ref}\ll f_0fref​≪f0​ — sampling a 5 GHz sinusoid at 100 MHz is severe undersampling, but we only care about the phase error, and the phase error is exactly what gets aliased down near DC — which is what we want. (The idea of using a sampling phase detector for microwave synthesis is itself old — the step-recovery-diode sampler has long been a staple of microwave instrumentation (external literature, not separately cited); what turned it into an integrated CMOS PLL with a full noise analysis is the Gao et al. 2009 paper cited above.)

Deriving KPDK_{PD}KPD​. At lock, ω0Tref=2πN\omega_0 T_{ref}=2\pi Nω0​Tref​=2πN (the VCO completes an integer number of cycles per reference period). Let the kkk-th reference edge land at tk=kTref+δtkt_k=kT_{ref}+\delta t_ktk​=kTref​+δtk​ (δtk\delta t_kδtk​ being the reference's own timing error, in s), with VCO output V(t)=Asin⁡(ω0t+ϕout)V(t)=A\sin(\omega_0 t+\phi_{out})V(t)=Asin(ω0​t+ϕout​). The sampled voltage:

Vk=Asin⁡(ω0kTref+ω0 δtk+ϕout)=Asin⁡(2πNk⏟integer cycles, drops out+ ϕout+ω0 δtk)≈A(ϕout+ω0 δtk)V_k=A\sin\big(\omega_0 kT_{ref}+\omega_0\,\delta t_k+\phi_{out}\big) =A\sin\big(\underbrace{2\pi Nk}_{\text{integer cycles, drops out}}+\ \phi_{out}+\omega_0\,\delta t_k\big) \approx A\big(\phi_{out}+\omega_0\,\delta t_k\big)Vk​=Asin(ω0​kTref​+ω0​δtk​+ϕout​)=Asin(integer cycles, drops out2πNk​​+ ϕout​+ω0​δtk​)≈A(ϕout​+ω0​δtk​)

Step 3: deriving the in-band advantage — divider term vanishes, CP noise no longer ×N2\times N^2×N2​

Line up steps 1 and 2. Take the same noise current PSD SiS_{i}Si​ (A²/Hz) and refer it to output phase:

Sϕ,outclassic=N2Kcp2 Si=(2πNIcp)2Si,Sϕ,outSS=Si(gmA)2,S_{\phi,out}^{\text{classic}}=\frac{N^2}{K_{cp}^2}\,S_i=\Big(\frac{2\pi N}{I_{cp}}\Big)^2 S_i, \qquad S_{\phi,out}^{\text{SS}}=\frac{S_i}{(g_m A)^2},Sϕ,outclassic​=Kcp2​N2​Si​=(Icp​2πN​)2Si​,Sϕ,outSS​=(gm​A)2Si​​,

Dividing the two gives sub-sampling's suppression ratio for front-end current noise:

  • Dimension check: 2πNgmA/Icp2\pi N g_m A/I_{cp}2πNgm​A/Icp​ = dimensionless×(A/rad)/(A) — rad is dimensionless, so the whole ratio is dimensionless ✓, and the log is valid.
  • Illustrative numbers (N=50N=50N=50, gmA=2.5g_m A=2.5gm​A=2.5 mA/rad, Icp=1I_{cp}=1Icp​=1 mA): 33.98+23.92=57.933.98+23.92=57.933.98+23.92=57.9 dB.
  • The first term is exactly the "∼20log⁡10N\sim20\log_{10}N∼20log10​N removal of the divider multiplier": CP/PD noise in sub-sampling is no longer amplified by ×N2\times N^2×N2 (external standard result, Gao et al. 2009, cited above).   • What remains of the in-band floor is ≈N2Sref\approx N^2S_{ref}≈N2Sref​ + the sampler's own noise — it becomes reference-limited. To go further, the only options are a cleaner reference or a higher freff_{ref}fref​ to lower NNN (there's no free lunch, per rule 1 of clock_chain_budget).

ILCM comparison (same "kick the divider/CP out" goal, a completely different mechanism): the injection-locked clock multiplier (ILCM) also drives the in-band floor to reference-limited, but by a different route — the sub-sampling PLL is a continuous-time closed loop (the divider is reduced to an auxiliary FLL; sampling the zero crossing is the phase detector), while the ILCM is discrete-time, open-loop injection (there is no PFD/CP/divider at all — a pulse generator directly "pulls" the phase toward the reference). The in-band bookkeeping converges to the same destination by different paths: sub-sampling gives N2Sref∣Hlp∣2N^2S_{ref}\vert H_{lp}\vert^2N2Sref​∣Hlp​∣2 (the divider term disappears, CP is no longer ×N2\times N^2×N2); the ILCM gives N2Sref∣Href∣2N^2S_{ref}\vert H_{ref}\vert^2N2Sref​∣Href​∣2 with Href=β/(1−(1−β)z−1)H_{ref}=\beta/(1-(1-\beta)z^{-1})Href​=β/(1−(1−β)z−1), a first-order discrete-time low-pass (β\betaβ = the realignment factor) — the same ×N2\times N^2×N2, two completely different ways of kicking the divider out. The full derivation (lock range, β\betaβ, discrete-time noise shaping) is on subharmonic_injection.

Step 4: the ISF connection — sampling the zero crossing = sampling where ∣Γ∣\lvert\Gamma\rvert∣Γ∣ is largest​

This step is where this site's main thread and sub-sampling elegantly intersect. The ISF of an ideal LC oscillator with output V=Acos⁡(ω0t)V=A\cos(\omega_0 t)V=Acos(ω0​t) is Γ(ω0τ)=−sin⁡(ω0τ)\Gamma(\omega_0\tau)=-\sin(\omega_0\tau)Γ(ω0​τ)=−sin(ω0​τ) (lab_02, corresponding to [P1]'s LC example) — ∣Γ∣\lvert\Gamma\rvert∣Γ∣ is largest at the zero crossing (=1) and zero at the peak. The SSPD happens to fire exactly at the zero crossing, so this single location has two faces:

(a) Maximum efficiency at reading phase, and naturally AM-rejecting. At the zero crossing, V=Asin⁡(ϕerr)≈AϕerrV=A\sin(\phi_{err})\approx A\phi_{err}V=Asin(ϕerr​)≈Aϕerr​: the voltage carries phase information "one-to-one" (the gain AAA V/rad is the maximum anywhere on the sinusoid); meanwhile an amplitude error δA\delta AδA enters as δAsin⁡(ϕerr)≈δA⋅ϕerr\delta A\sin(\phi_{err})\approx\delta A\cdot\phi_{err}δAsin(ϕerr​)≈δA⋅ϕerr​ — a second-order small quantity, so it doesn't enter at first order. Conversely, if sampling happened at the peak: ∂V/∂ϕ=0\partial V/\partial\phi=0∂V/∂ϕ=0 (no phase readout at all), while δA\delta AδA enters at full strength. This matches exactly the decomposition in phase_vs_amplitude_noise: the zero crossing is a "pure phase" window. The geometry of sampling is the same thread as the aperture math: step 1 of adc_aperture_jitter, "sampling error = slope × timing error," is noise on the ADC page (clock jitter dirtying the sample), but on this page it's flipped around and used as the signal (the reference edge's timing offset is converted into a measurable voltage via the slope Aω0A\omega_0Aω0​) — the same equation, one side is a liability, the other a phase detector.

(b) Also maximum efficiency at hitting phase — kickback becomes a spur. Every time the sampling switch closes, it exchanges a small packet of charge Δq\Delta qΔq with the VCO node (charge sharing / switch feedthrough). Using [P1]'s operational ISF definition (canonical formula 5):

Δϕ=Γ(ω0τ)qmax Δq\Delta\phi=\frac{\Gamma(\omega_0\tau)}{q_{max}}\,\Delta qΔϕ=qmax​Γ(ω0​τ)​Δq

Hitting at the zero crossing → ∣Γ∣=1\lvert\Gamma\rvert=1∣Γ∣=1 (maximum) → every kick lands with full force. Illustrative (following the scale of canonical example A, taking ∣Γ∣=1\lvert\Gamma\rvert=1∣Γ∣=1): Δq=1\Delta q=1Δq=1 fC, qmax=1q_{max}=1qmax​=1 pC → Δϕ=1\Delta\phi=1Δϕ=1 mrad per reference period. This disturbance is deterministic, periodic at freff_{ref}fref​, so it isn't a continuous spectrum but a reference spur (canonical section 10.2's small-angle PM: a residual phase ripple of fundamental amplitude ϕp\phi_pϕp​ produces a sideband =20log⁡10(ϕp/2)=20\log_{10}(\phi_p/2)=20log10​(ϕp​/2) dBc; ϕp=1\phi_p=1ϕp​=1 mrad → −66.0-66.0−66.0 dBc, and if the loop suppresses it to 0.5 mrad → −72.0-72.0−72.0 dBc, illustrative). The same ∣Γ∣max\lvert\Gamma\rvert_{max}∣Γ∣max​ that gives you maximum phase-detector gain also gives you maximum kickback damage — this is sub-sampling's core trade-off, unpacked in the next step. (Distinguishing spurs from random PN is covered in measurement_and_spurs.)

Step 5: the price — aliasing, reference spur, lock range​

(1) Aliasing: wideband noise folds into ±fref/2\pm f_{ref}/2±fref​/2. The SSPD is a system that samples at freff_{ref}fref​ (the same math as the ADC in adc_aperture_jitter). The wideband voltage noise at the sampler input (thermal noise of the VCO buffer, PSD SvS_vSv​ V²/Hz, bandwidth Bn≫frefB_n\gg f_{ref}Bn​≫fref​) folds back into the single-sided band fref/2f_{ref}/2fref​/2 with power conserved:

Sv,fold=Sv Bnfref/2=2SvBnfref⟹Sϕ,fold=Sv,foldA2  [rad2/Hz]S_{v,fold}=\frac{S_v\,B_n}{f_{ref}/2}=\frac{2S_v B_n}{f_{ref}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad S_{\phi,fold}=\frac{S_{v,fold}}{A^2}\ \ [\text{rad}^2/\text{Hz}]Sv,fold​=fref​/2Sv​Bn​​=fref​2Sv​Bn​​⟹Sϕ,fold​=A2Sv,fold​​  [rad2/Hz]

Dimension check: [V2/Hz]×[Hz]÷[Hz]=[V2/Hz][\text{V}^2/\text{Hz}]\times[\text{Hz}]\div[\text{Hz}]=[\text{V}^2/\text{Hz}][V2/Hz]×[Hz]÷[Hz]=[V2/Hz] ✓; dividing by A2A^2A2 [V²/rad²] gives rad²/Hz ✓.

(Dividing by A2A^2A2: the voltage-to-phase gain at the zero crossing is AAA V/rad.) For the sampling capacitor itself, the total noise power is the famous kT/CskT/C_skT/Cs​ (independent of bandwidth); once folded, Sv=2kT/(Csfref)S_v=2kT/(C_s f_{ref})Sv​=2kT/(Cs​fref​). Illustrative: Cs=100C_s=100Cs​=100 fF → kT/Cs=204 μ\sqrt{kT/C_s}=204\ \mukT/Cs​​=204 μV rms → L=−147.8\mathcal{L}=-147.8L=−147.8 dBc/Hz — much lower than the −126-126−126 reference floor (good news), but you have to account for folding before buying a wideband buffer: every doubling of BnB_nBn​ adds 3 dB to this term.

(2) The reference-spur see-saw. The kickback from step 4(b) lands exactly where ∣Γ∣\lvert\Gamma\rvert∣Γ∣ is largest, so the spur is inherently worse than in a classic PLL. Every countermeasure is a trade: adding an isolation buffer (spur ↓, but the buffer's own noise folds in per (1), and it costs power); shrinking CsC_sCs​ (Δq\Delta qΔq ↓ → spur ↓, but kT/CskT/C_skT/Cs​ ↑ — noise and spur sit on opposite ends of the same see-saw); dummy-sampler cancellation. The same team's follow-up paper deals specifically with spur reduction: X. Gao, E. A. M. Klumperink, G. Socci, M. Bohsali, and B. Nauta, "Spur Reduction Techniques for Phase-Locked Loops Exploiting a Sub-Sampling Phase Detector," IEEE J. Solid-State Circuits, vol. 45, no. 9, pp. 1809–1821, Sep. 2010 (external literature, not among the five source PDFs; TODO: manual verification needed — volume/issue/page numbers should be manually verified before citing in a formal document).

(3) Lock range: sin⁡\sinsin can't tell which cycle you're on. sin⁡\sinsin is 2π2\pi2π-periodic — every zero crossing of the VCO looks identical to the SSPD, so the SSPD has zero discrimination against "frequency error," and there's no hardware that defines NNN at all: any integer kkk satisfying f0=k freff_0=k\,f_{ref}f0​=kfref​ is a valid lock point (harmonic lock — locking to the wrong harmonic). So an SSPLL always pairs with an auxiliary FLL (frequency-locked loop: a conventional ÷N + PFD/CP) responsible for pulling the frequency close to the correct NfrefN f_{ref}Nfref​; it carries a dead zone — once locked, the phase error is tiny, the auxiliary loop goes completely silent, and the divider's noise never enters the main loop. In an SSPLL, the number NNN is defined by the auxiliary loop.

(4) Effect on the optimal loop BW (tying back to the budget page). Using the afn+b/fnaf_n+b/f_nafn​+b/fn​ toy model from pll_noise_budget: if the in-band floor aaa drops by 7.1 dB (power ×1/5.12\times1/5.12×1/5.12, the illustrative numbers from the worked example below) → the optimum fn\*∝b/af_n^\*\propto\sqrt{b/a}fn\*​∝b/a​ widens by 5.12=2.26×\sqrt{5.12}=2.26\times5.12​=2.26×, and the minimum integrated jitter ∝(ab)1/4\propto(ab)^{1/4}∝(ab)1/4 improves by 5.121/4=1.5×5.12^{1/4}=1.5\times5.121/4=1.5× — a lower floor isn't just a lower floor, it also lets you open the loop bandwidth wider and suppress more of the VCO, so the total-jitter payoff is larger than the floor's dB number alone suggests.

Python verification (runs as-is; # -> shows actual output):

import numpy as np
N, f_ref = 50, 100e6
f0 = N*f_ref                                        # 5 GHz
padd = lambda *L: 10*np.log10(sum(10**(x/10) for x in L))
# --- (a) classic charge-pump PLL: the three in-band terms (illustrative) ---
L_ref_out = -160.0 + 20*np.log10(N)
print(round(L_ref_out, 2))                          # -> -126.02 (ref ×N²)
K_cp = 1e-3/(2*np.pi)                               # I_cp=1 mA -> 159.2 uA/rad
Si = (4e-12)**2                                     # (4 pA/√Hz)²
L_cp = 10*np.log10(0.5*Si/K_cp**2*N**2)
print(round(L_cp, 2))                               # -> -121.03 (CP, ÷K_cp² then ×N²)
L_div_out = -160.0 + 20*np.log10(N)
print(round(L_div_out, 2))                          # -> -126.02 (divider ×N²)
L_classic = padd(L_ref_out, L_cp, L_div_out)
print(round(L_classic, 1))                          # -> -118.9 (CP dominates)
# --- (b) sub-sampling PLL (illustrative) ---
K_ss = 5e-3*0.5                                     # g_m·A = 2.5 mA/rad (referred to output phase)
print(round(20*np.log10(K_ss/(K_cp/N)), 1))         # -> 57.9 (= 33.98 + 23.92 dB)
L_gm = 10*np.log10(0.5*Si/K_ss**2)
print(round(L_gm, 2))                               # -> -178.93 (same i_n, ÷K_SS²)
L_smp = 10*np.log10(0.5*(2*1.380649e-23*300/(100e-15*f_ref))/0.5**2)
print(round(L_smp, 2))                              # -> -147.81 (kT/C folded into f_ref/2)
L_ss = padd(L_ref_out, L_gm, L_smp)
print(round(L_ss, 2))                               # -> -125.99 (reference-limited)
print(round(L_classic - L_ss, 1))                   # -> 7.1 (in-band improvement, dB)
# --- (c) convert the in-band portion into jitter (brick-wall 10 kHz–1 MHz) ---
for L in (L_classic, L_ss):
    st = np.sqrt(2*10**(L/10)*(1e6-1e4))/(2*np.pi*f0)
    print(round(st*1e15, 1))                        # -> 50.9 / 22.5 (fs)

Honesty note: these illustrative numbers were chosen so the classic architecture is "CP-dominated" and sub-sampling is "reference-dominated" — a deliberately typical teaching scenario; in a real design ini_nin​, IcpI_{cp}Icp​, duty cycle, and auxiliary-loop residuals would all shift the relative ranking of the terms, but the structural result — "the divider term vanishes and the CP term is no longer ×N2\times N^2×N2" — does not change (Gao et al. 2009's measurements indeed show the in-band floor approaching the reference-limited value).

Connection to SerDes​

In a SerDes sampling-clock jitter budget, the in-band floor is often the dominant term (clock_chain_budget's worked chain: 65.9% comes from the in-band floor raised by ×N2\times N^2×N2). This page's illustrative numbers compress the in-band portion from 50.9 fs to 22.5 fs (2.26×2.26\times2.26×), and the optimal loop BW can then also widen by 2.26×2.26\times2.26× to suppress more of the VCO — a direct credit to serdes_clocking_connection's eye/BER accounting (RJ overhead =2Q−1σt=2Q^{-1}\sigma_t=2Q−1σt​). On the cost side: the reference spur is deterministic jitter (DJ, see dj_dual_dirac), which shows up on the eye diagram as a dual peak rather than a Gaussian tail — the "lower RJ, higher DJ risk" trade that sub-sampling buys is exactly what the system level needs to watch.

Key takeaways​

  • Classic CP-PLL front-end noise referred to output: Sϕ,out=N2Si/Kcp2S_{\phi,out}=N^2S_i/K_{cp}^2Sϕ,out​=N2Si​/Kcp2​, Kcp=Icp/2πK_{cp}=I_{cp}/2\piKcp​=Icp​/2π — small gain in the denominator, then ×N2\times N^2×N2 — this is why the in-band floor is stuck at CP+divider (the microscopic version of pll_noise_budget).
  • Sub-sampling: the reference edge directly samples the VCO sinusoid's zero crossing, giving KPD=AK_{PD}=AKPD​=A V/rad (slope Aω0A\omega_0Aω0​ ÷ ω0\omega_0ω0​), paired with a gmg_mgm​ stage to give KSS=gmAK_{SS}=g_mAKSS​=gm​A A/rad — phase detection referred to output phase, no ÷N.
  • The advantage = 20log⁡10N20\log_{10}N20log10​N (removal of the divider multiplier) + 20log⁡10(2πgmA/Icp)20\log_{10}(2\pi g_mA/I_{cp})20log10​(2πgm​A/Icp​) (gain bonus); illustrative 33.98+23.92=57.933.98+23.92=57.933.98+23.92=57.9 dB; the divider noise term vanishes entirely (external standard result, Gao et al. JSSC 2009).
  • Reference ×N2\times N^2×N2 is present in both — the ×N\times N×N is hidden in the sampling slope (ω0δt=Nωrefδt\omega_0\delta t=N\omega_{ref}\delta tω0​δt=Nωref​δt), an intrinsic property of frequency multiplication, not the divider's fault. The SSPLL floor is therefore reference-limited.
  • ISF duality: sampling the zero crossing = sampling where ∣Γ∣\lvert\Gamma\rvert∣Γ∣ is largest — maximum gain for reading phase (AAA V/rad, AM-resistant), and maximum severity for hitting phase via kickback (Δϕ=ΓΔq/qmax\Delta\phi=\Gamma\Delta q/q_{max}Δϕ=ΓΔq/qmax​, [P1]'s operational definition) → the reference spur is inherently worse; noise (kT/C ↑) and spur (Δq\Delta qΔq ↓) share the same CsC_sCs​ see-saw.
  • Aliasing: wideband noise at the sampler input folds into ±fref/2\pm f_{ref}/2±fref​/2 (Sfold=2SvBn/frefS_{fold}=2S_vB_n/f_{ref}Sfold​=2Sv​Bn​/fref​); the kT/C version is illustratively −147.8-147.8−147.8 dBc/Hz.
  • The SSPD can't tell which cycle it's on (harmonic lock) → needs an auxiliary FLL (÷N + PFD/CP + dead zone) to define NNN and manage acquisition.
  • Worked example (illustrative): in-band floor −118.9→−125.99-118.9\to-125.99−118.9→−125.99 dBc/Hz (+7.1+7.1+7.1 dB), in-band jitter 50.9→22.550.9\to22.550.9→22.5 fs; the lower floor also lets the optimal loop BW widen by 2.26×2.26\times2.26×, gaining another 1.5×1.5\times1.5× on total jitter.
