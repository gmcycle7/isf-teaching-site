From ISF to SerDes Clocking

Prerequisites: psd_phase_noise_jitter (SϕS_\phiSϕ​, L\mathcal{L}L, the four jitter "dialects," and phase↔time conversion), tank_swing (L∝Γrms2/qmax2\mathcal{L}\propto\Gamma_{rms}^2/q_{max}^2L∝Γrms2​/qmax2​ sets the VCO's own noise), lc_vs_ring (accumulated jitter σΔt=κΔt\sigma_{\Delta t}=\kappa\sqrt{\Delta t}σΔt​=κΔt​ and the LC/ring trade-off) | Next: pll_noise_budget, exercises

This is the capstone page of Chapter 06: it connects everything said so far about ISF / phase noise to what a SerDes (serializer/deserializer, high-speed serial transceiver) designer looks at every day — clock jitter, eye opening, BER (bit error rate). We derive, step by step, the two phase→time conversions, clarify how to choose the integration bandwidth, the difference between RJ and DJ, and why a CDR/PLL behaves like a high-pass filter that removes the VCO's close-in noise — which is why "the same VCO behaves completely differently under different loops."

Physical intuition (conclusion first): an oscillator's phase noise is "the phase jittering." What a SerDes designer cares about is "how much the edge jitters on the time axis" and "the probability that the sampling instant lands near the center of the eye." Phase jitter → time jitter (Δt=Δϕ/2πf0\Delta t=\Delta\phi/2\pi f_0Δt=Δϕ/2πf0​) → the sampling point drifts off the eye center → the eye narrows → BER worsens. But not all phase noise, regardless of frequency, is harmful: the CDR/PLL tracks slow phase drift (low-frequency noise is absorbed by the loop), leaving only the fast noise the loop cannot follow to become jitter. So "integration bandwidth" and "loop bandwidth" are the two central knobs of SerDes clocking.

Step 1: phase → time (a single phase error)​

How does an oscillator's excess phase Δϕ\Delta\phiΔϕ turn into an edge timing error? One period T=1/f0T=1/f_0T=1/f0​ corresponds to 2π2\pi2π rad of phase, so phase and time are proportional (notation, Eq. 17):

 Δt=Δϕ2πf0 \boxed{\ \Delta t=\frac{\Delta\phi}{2\pi f_0}\ } Δt=2πf0​Δϕ​ ​

Step-by-step derivation: the signal is V=Acos⁡(2πf0t+ϕ)V=A\cos(2\pi f_0 t+\phi)V=Acos(2πf0​t+ϕ). Treat a perturbation Δϕ\Delta\phiΔϕ of ϕ\phiϕ as a time shift Δt\Delta tΔt: requiring 2πf0(t+Δt)=2πf0t+Δϕ2\pi f_0(t+\Delta t)=2\pi f_0 t+\Delta\phi2πf0​(t+Δt)=2πf0​t+Δϕ and solving gives 2πf0 Δt=Δϕ2\pi f_0\,\Delta t=\Delta\phi2πf0​Δt=Δϕ, i.e.

Δt=Δϕ2πf0.\Delta t=\frac{\Delta\phi}{2\pi f_0}.Δt=2πf0​Δϕ​.
  • Dimension check: [rad]/[rad/s]=[s][\text{rad}]/[\text{rad/s}]=[\text{s}][rad]/[rad/s]=[s] ✓ (2πf02\pi f_02πf0​ has units of rad/s, not Hz).
  • Intuition (5 GHz): 1 mrad → 31.8 fs; 1 rad → 31.8 ps (≈ T/6.28T/6.28T/6.28, sensible).

Step 2: phase PSD → rms jitter (needs an integral)​

A single Δϕ\Delta\phiΔϕ is not useful — jitter is the sum of phase noise across all frequencies. First integrate the phase PSD to get phase variance (Eq. 18):

σϕ2=∫f1f2Sϕ(f) df\sigma_\phi^2=\int_{f_1}^{f_2}S_\phi(f)\,dfσϕ2​=∫f1​f2​​Sϕ​(f)df

Then use Step 1 to convert rms phase to rms time (Eq. 19):

 σt=σϕ2πf0=12πf0∫f1f2Sϕ(f) df \boxed{\ \sigma_t=\frac{\sigma_\phi}{2\pi f_0}=\frac{1}{2\pi f_0}\sqrt{\int_{f_1}^{f_2}S_\phi(f)\,df}\ } σt​=2πf0​σϕ​​=2πf0​1​∫f1​f2​​Sϕ​(f)df​ ​

Step-by-step explanation:

  1. Why integrate: Sϕ(f)S_\phi(f)Sϕ​(f) is "phase power per Hz" (rad²/Hz). Phase jitter at different offset frequencies is independent, so the powers add — integrating over fff gives the total phase variance σϕ2\sigma_\phi^2σϕ2​ (rad²).
  2. Dimension check: [rad2/Hz]⋅[Hz]=[rad2][\text{rad}^2/\text{Hz}]\cdot[\text{Hz}]=[\text{rad}^2][rad2/Hz]⋅[Hz]=[rad2] ✓ → take the square root to get rad.
  3. Convert to time: divide by 2πf02\pi f_02πf0​ (rad/s) → s.
  4. Connecting to measurement: a phase-noise analyzer reports L(f)\mathcal{L}(f)L(f) (dBc/Hz); use the small-angle approximation Sϕ=2⋅10L/10S_\phi=2\cdot10^{\mathcal{L}/10}Sϕ​=2⋅10L/10 (the factor-of-2 for single-sided → double-sided, Eq. 16) to convert L\mathcal{L}L to SϕS_\phiSϕ​ before integrating.

Step 4: how jitter eats into eye opening and BER​

The SerDes receiver samples at the center of each bit. Let the bit period (UI, unit interval) be TbT_bTb​. Timing jitter σt\sigma_tσt​ at the sampling instant directly "eats into" the eye's horizontal opening from both sides. For the case of RJ (Gaussian) only, the BER at an offset ttt from the eye center is a bathtub curve (standard SerDes model, §10.2 of the spec):

BER(t)=12[Q ⁣(UI/2−tσt)+Q ⁣(UI/2+tσt)],Q(x)=12 erfc ⁣(x2)\text{BER}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left[Q\!\left(\frac{\text{UI}/2-t}{\sigma_t}\right)+Q\!\left(\frac{\text{UI}/2+t}{\sigma_t}\right)\right],\qquad Q(x)=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt2}\right)BER(t)=21​[Q(σt​UI/2−t​)+Q(σt​UI/2+t​)],Q(x)=21​erfc(2​x​)

  • How to read it: the two QQQ terms are, respectively, the probability that "the left edge (at −UI/2-\text{UI}/2−UI/2) jitters past the sample point on the right" and that "the right edge (at +UI/2+\text{UI}/2+UI/2) jitters past the sample point on the left." At the exact eye center t=0t=0t=0 the two terms are equal and BER is lowest (the bottom of the bathtub).

  • Solving for eye opening from BER: to meet a target BER (e.g. 10−1210^{-12}10−12), the one-sided margin at the bottom (t=0t=0t=0) must satisfy UI/2−Q−1(BER)⋅σt>0\text{UI}/2-Q^{-1}(\text{BER})\cdot\sigma_t > 0UI/2−Q−1(BER)⋅σt​>0. With Q−1(10−12)≈7.03Q^{-1}(10^{-12})\approx7.03Q−1(10−12)≈7.03:

  • Eye horizontal opening (one-sided) ≈0.5 UI−(opposite-side ISI)−Q−1(BER)⋅σt\approx0.5\,\text{UI}-(\text{opposite-side ISI})-Q^{-1}(\text{BER})\cdot\sigma_t≈0.5UI−(opposite-side ISI)−Q−1(BER)⋅σt​, where Q−1(BER)Q^{-1}(\text{BER})Q−1(BER) is the inverse Gaussian Q-function value for the target BER (e.g. BER =10−12=10^{-12}=10−12 → Q−1≈7.03Q^{-1}\approx7.03Q−1≈7.03, total RJ ≈ 14σt14\sigma_t14σt​ peak-to-peak).

  • Intuition: RJ is Gaussian and unbounded, so the stricter the BER target (the smaller the probability), the more margin must be reserved (larger QQQ). Each increment of σt\sigma_tσt​ eats ∼Qσt\sim Q\sigma_t∼Qσt​ off each side of the eye.

  • Order of magnitude: in the example above, σt=448\sigma_t=448σt​=448 fs; at f0=5f_0=5f0​=5 GHz, if the data rate is 10 Gb/s (Tb=100T_b=100Tb​=100 ps), the RJ overhead for BER 10−1210^{-12}10−12 is ≈14×448 fs=6.3\approx14\times448\,\text{fs}=6.3≈14×448fs=6.3 ps =0.063=0.063=0.063 UI — clock RJ alone eats 6.3% of the eye. This is why high-speed links are so sensitive to VCO phase noise.

Step 6: CDR/PLL high-pass filtering of VCO phase noise (the key point)​

This is the single most important idea on this page, and the answer to "why the free-running σΔt=κΔt\sigma_{\Delta t}=\kappa\sqrt{\Delta t}σΔt​=κΔt​ does not grow without bound in practice."

  • Put the VCO inside a PLL (locked to a clean reference) or a CDR (locked to data edges), and the loop tracks the VCO's slow phase drift: as long as the drift is slow enough (offset frequency below the loop bandwidth fBWf_{BW}fBW​), the loop corrects it.
  • The result is that the VCO's own phase noise experiences a high-pass transfer function:
∣HVCO→out(f)∣2≈f2f2+fBW2⇒{below fBW: suppressed (loop keeps up)above fBW: passes unchanged\big|H_{VCO\to out}(f)\big|^2\approx\frac{f^2}{f^2+f_{BW}^2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\begin{cases}\text{below }f_{BW}:\text{ suppressed (loop keeps up)}\\ \text{above }f_{BW}:\text{ passes unchanged}\end{cases}​HVCO→out​(f)​2≈f2+fBW2​f2​⇒{below fBW​: suppressed (loop keeps up)above fBW​: passes unchanged​
  • Conversely, the reference clock (or the CDR's input data-edge) noise experiences a low-pass: low frequencies pass unchanged, high frequencies are filtered out.
  • Design consequences:
    • The integration lower limit f1f_1f1​ should be taken near the loop bandwidth (not DC) — because VCO noise below fBWf_{BW}fBW​ has already been absorbed by the loop. This directly answers "how to choose the integration bandwidth": closed-loop jitter is integrated from fBWf_{BW}fBW​ to f2f_2f2​ (Nyquist or half the data rate).
    • Accumulated jitter is truncated: the free-running random walk (∝Δt\propto\sqrt{\Delta t}∝Δt​, energy concentrated at very low frequency) falls exactly in the high-pass stopband → it is suppressed into a bounded tracking error. So once locked, it no longer drifts without bound.
    • Loop bandwidth is a trade-off: raising fBWf_{BW}fBW​ → filters out more VCO close-in noise (good), but lets more reference/input noise and the loop's own noise through (bad). The optimal fBWf_{BW}fBW​ sits near the crossover point of the VCO-noise and reference-noise curves.

⚠️ The PLL/CDR high-pass/low-pass transfer function is standard PLL theory (not in the five downloaded PDFs; supplemented from standard literature, e.g. Gardner, Razavi, Best). Step 7: practical intuition for TX PLL / RX PLL / LC-VCO / ring-VCO​

ScenarioIntuitionLC or ring?
TX PLL (generates the transmit clock)Directly sets transmit jitter; usually narrow loop BW (absorbs ref noise), so VCO close-in noise mattersHigh-speed lanes use LC-VCO (low phase noise); jitter requirements are strict
RX CDR (recovers clock from data)Loop BW is set by data/jitter tolerance; high-passes away VCO close-inCommonly uses ring-VCO (wide tuning, multi-phase, small area — and close-in is filtered by the CDR, so it is less fatal)
LC-VCOLow Γrms/qmax\Gamma_{rms}/q_{max}Γrms​/qmax​, low phase noise, slow drift; large area, narrow tuningTX, reference, high performance
ring-VCOWorse phase noise, faster jitter accumulation; but wide tuning, naturally multi-phase, small area; close-in is suppressed once inside a fast loopRX CDR, low-power/area-sensitive, multi-phase needs
  • Core trade-off: the ring's weakness (close-in 1/f³, fast random walk) falls exactly in the loop's high-pass stopband — if the loop bandwidth is wide enough, the ring-VCO's close-in weakness is largely compensated. This is why many RX CDRs use ring instead of LC.
  • Conversely, a TX PLL often uses a narrow loop to filter out reference spurs, so VCO close-in noise appears directly at the output → LC is preferred.

Key takeaways​

  • Δt=Δϕ/(2πf0)\Delta t=\Delta\phi/(2\pi f_0)Δt=Δϕ/(2πf0​); σt=12πf0∫f1f2Sϕdf\sigma_t=\frac{1}{2\pi f_0}\sqrt{\int_{f_1}^{f_2}S_\phi df}σt​=2πf0​1​∫f1​f2​​Sϕ​df​ (1/f² is dominated by the lower integration limit).
  • Example C: −100-100−100 dBc/Hz @ 1 MHz, 5 GHz, 1/f², 1→100 MHz → σϕ=14.07\sigma_\phi=14.07σϕ​=14.07 mrad, σt=447.9\sigma_t=447.9σt​=447.9 fs.
  • Jitter eats the eye from both sides: ∼Qσt\sim Q\sigma_t∼Qσt​/side; at 10 Gb/s, BER 10−1210^{-12}10−12: 448 fs RJ ≈ 0.063 UI.
  • RJ (Gaussian, from phase noise, governed by ISF) vs. DJ (bounded, ISI/crosstalk, invisible to ISF); TJ === DJpp+2Q⋅_{pp}+2Q\cdotpp​+2Q⋅RJrms_{rms}rms​.
  • CDR/PLL is high-pass to VCO noise: close-in/accumulated jitter is suppressed → integration lower limit ≈ fBWf_{BW}fBW​.
  • Practice: TX PLL favors LC-VCO (close-in matters); RX CDR often uses ring-VCO (close-in filtered by the loop).
