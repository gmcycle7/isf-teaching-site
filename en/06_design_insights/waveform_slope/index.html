Waveform slope and phase sensitivity

Prerequisites: impulse_to_phase_shift (Δϕ=Γ Δq/qmax\Delta\phi=\Gamma\,\Delta q/q_{max}Δϕ=ΓΔq/qmax​ operational definition and qmax=CVmaxq_{max}=CV_{max}qmax​=CVmax​), lti_vs_ltv (why the same impulse at different phases has different effect) | Next: symmetry, tank_swing

This page answers a very practical question, one you'll use during layout and bias design: at which phase of the waveform does the same lump of noise charge do the most damage? Intuitively you might guess "the peak — highest, most sensitive" — it's the opposite. ISF magnitude is, roughly, inversely proportional to waveform slope: where the slope is small (near the peak), ∣Γ∣|\Gamma|∣Γ∣ is large and phase error is easiest to push; where the slope is large (at the ZC, zero crossing), noise is instead mostly converted into amplitude and pulled back.

Physical intuition (conclusion first): phase is "how fast the state point runs along the tangent of the limit cycle." At the peak, the state point moves "slowly, flatly" (dV/dt≈0dV/dt\approx0dV/dt≈0); nudge it sideways and it takes a long time along the time axis to return to where it would have been — equivalent to a large phase shift. At the ZC, the state point is "moving at full speed" (dV/dtdV/dtdV/dt maximal); the same sideways nudge is quickly absorbed back onto the trajectory, leaving almost no phase (it mostly becomes a decaying amplitude perturbation). So "phases with small slope" are the danger zone.

Step 1: the inverse relation between ISF and slope​

Return to the operational definition of impulse→phase ([P1] Eq.(10)–(11), p.182):

Δϕ=Γ(ω0τ)qmax Δq\Delta\phi=\frac{\Gamma(\omega_0\tau)}{q_{max}}\,\Delta qΔϕ=qmax​Γ(ω0​τ)​Δq

A lump of charge Δq\Delta qΔq produces a voltage jump ΔV=Δq/C\Delta V=\Delta q/CΔV=Δq/C ([P1] Eq.(9)). Translate this voltage jump into a "time-axis advance/delay": the waveform passes through this point with slope V˙=dV/dt\dot V=dV/dtV˙=dV/dt, and raising the voltage by ΔV\Delta VΔV is equivalent to shifting the waveform in time by

Δt≈ΔVV˙=ΔqC V˙.\Delta t\approx\frac{\Delta V}{\dot V}=\frac{\Delta q}{C\,\dot V}.Δt≈V˙ΔV​=CV˙Δq​.
  • Unit check: [V]/[V/s]=[s][\text{V}]/[\text{V/s}]=[\text{s}][V]/[V/s]=[s] ✓.
  • Converting to phase, Δϕ=ω0Δt\Delta\phi=\omega_0\Delta tΔϕ=ω0​Δt:
Δϕ=ω0ΔqC V˙⇒Γ∝1V˙.\Delta\phi=\omega_0\frac{\Delta q}{C\,\dot V}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \Gamma\propto\frac{1}{\dot V}.Δϕ=ω0​CV˙Δq​⇒Γ∝V˙1​.

Step-by-step algebra: aligning this "shift approximation" with the ISF operational definition to see exactly what Γ\GammaΓ is proportional to. Set the Δϕ\Delta\phiΔϕ from the above equation equal to the operational-definition expression Δϕ=Γ Δq/qmax\Delta\phi=\Gamma\,\Delta q/q_{max}Δϕ=ΓΔq/qmax​:

Γqmax Δq=ω0 ΔqC V˙Γ=qmax ω0C V˙(cancel Δq on both sides)Γ=(C Vmax) ω0C V˙=ω0 VmaxV˙(substitute qmax=C Vmax)=VmaxV˙/ω0=VmaxdV/dθ(V˙=ω0 dV/dθ).\begin{aligned} \frac{\Gamma}{q_{max}}\,\Delta q&=\omega_0\,\frac{\Delta q}{C\,\dot V} \\[4pt] \Gamma&=\frac{q_{max}\,\omega_0}{C\,\dot V} \qquad(\text{cancel }\Delta q\text{ on both sides}) \\[4pt] \Gamma&=\frac{(C\,V_{max})\,\omega_0}{C\,\dot V}=\frac{\omega_0\,V_{max}}{\dot V} \qquad(\text{substitute }q_{max}=C\,V_{max}) \\[4pt] &=\frac{V_{max}}{\dot V/\omega_0}=\frac{V_{max}}{dV/d\theta}\qquad(\dot V=\omega_0\,dV/d\theta). \end{aligned}qmax​Γ​ΔqΓΓ​=ω0​CV˙Δq​=CV˙qmax​ω0​​(cancel Δq on both sides)=CV˙(CVmax​)ω0​​=V˙ω0​Vmax​​(substitute qmax​=CVmax​)=V˙/ω0​Vmax​​=dV/dθVmax​​(V˙=ω0​dV/dθ).​

  • What each step uses: lines 1→2 are pure algebra (cancel Δq\Delta qΔq); lines 2→3 use qmax=CVmaxq_{max}=C V_{max}qmax​=CVmax​ (the capacitance definition — CCC cancels automatically, which is why ISF does not depend on node capacitance, only on waveform shape); lines 3→4 convert the time-domain slope to a phase-domain slope V˙=ω0 dV/dθ\dot V=\omega_0\,dV/d\thetaV˙=ω0​dV/dθ (chain rule).

  • Meaning of the result: Γ=Vmax/(dV/dθ)\Gamma=V_{max}\big/(dV/d\theta)Γ=Vmax​/(dV/dθ) — ISF is proportional to VmaxV_{max}Vmax​ and inversely proportional to the waveform's slope with respect to phase dV/dθdV/d\thetadV/dθ.

  • Check against a sinusoid: V=Vmaxcos⁡θ⇒dV/dθ=−Vmaxsin⁡θV=V_{max}\cos\theta\Rightarrow dV/d\theta=-V_{max}\sin\thetaV=Vmax​cosθ⇒dV/dθ=−Vmax​sinθ, so Γ=Vmax/(−Vmaxsin⁡θ)=−1/sin⁡θ\Gamma=V_{max}/(-V_{max}\sin\theta)=-1/\sin\thetaΓ=Vmax​/(−Vmax​sinθ)=−1/sinθ. This "1/sin⁡1/\sin1/sin" is an artifact of the shift approximation and diverges where slope→0 (the peak); the rigorous LTV projection instead gives a bounded Γ=−sin⁡θ\Gamma=-\sin\thetaΓ=−sinθ (with Γ=0\Gamma=0Γ=0 at the peak). Both say "small slope → dangerous," but they differ on whether it diverges — the shift approximation is only qualitatively valid where slope is nonzero; for quantitative work use Γ=−sin⁡θ\Gamma=-\sin\thetaΓ=−sinθ.

  • Dimension check: [V]/[V]=[\text{V}]/[\text{V}]=[V]/[V]= dimensionless ✓ (Γ\GammaΓ must be dimensionless); the intermediate expression [C]⋅[rad/s][F]⋅[V/s]=[C][s−1][C/V][V/s]=[C][s−1][C][s−1]=\dfrac{[\text{C}]\cdot[\text{rad/s}]}{[\text{F}]\cdot[\text{V/s}]}=\dfrac{[\text{C}][\text{s}^{-1}]}{[\text{C/V}][\text{V/s}]} =\dfrac{[\text{C}][\text{s}^{-1}]}{[\text{C}][\text{s}^{-1}]}=[F]⋅[V/s][C]⋅[rad/s]​=[C/V][V/s][C][s−1]​=[C][s−1][C][s−1]​= dimensionless ✓ (rad does not count as a dimension).

  • Conclusion: ISF magnitude is inversely proportional to instantaneous slope V˙\dot VV˙. Large slope → small ∣Γ∣|\Gamma|∣Γ∣ (insensitive); small slope → large ∣Γ∣|\Gamma|∣Γ∣ (sensitive).

  • For an ideal sinusoid V=cos⁡θV=\cos\thetaV=cosθ, V˙∝−sin⁡θ\dot V\propto-\sin\thetaV˙∝−sinθ, so the "shift" approximation Γ(θ)∝1/sin⁡θ\Gamma(\theta)\propto1/\sin\thetaΓ(θ)∝1/sinθ only holds where slope is nonzero; the rigorous LC ISF is Γ(θ)=−sin⁡θ\Gamma(\theta)=-\sin\thetaΓ(θ)=−sinθ (Γ=0\Gamma=0Γ=0 at the peak θ=0\theta=0θ=0, ∣Γ∣|\Gamma|∣Γ∣ maximal at the ZC θ=π/2\theta=\pi/2θ=π/2).

The divergence problem now has its rigorous resolution (resolved): the 1/slope1/\text{slope}1/slope divergence at the peak has an official "parent formula" — the closed form of [P1]'s appendix (Eq.(37), p.193) Γ=f′/(f′ 2+f′′ 2)\Gamma=f'/(f'^{\,2}+f''^{\,2})Γ=f′/(f′2+f′′2): on transitions (f′ 2≫f′′ 2f'^{\,2}\gg f''^{\,2}f′2≫f′′2) it degenerates into this page's 1/f′1/f'1/f′ heuristic; near the peak the numerator f′→0f'\to0f′→0 while the denominator is held up by f′′ 2f''^{\,2}f′′2, so Γ→0\Gamma\to0Γ→0 — bounded, no divergence. Substituting f=cos⁡f=\cosf=cos gives the denominator sin⁡2+cos⁡2=1\sin^2+\cos^2=1sin2+cos2=1 and Γ=−sin⁡\Gamma=-\sinΓ=−sin exactly. For the full derivation, the verbatim transcription of [P1]'s three ISF calculation methods, and the numerical duel, see isf_from_waveform.

A common direction confusion needs to be spelled out here: the "shift" picture above, "Δt=ΔV/V˙\Delta t=\Delta V/\dot VΔt=ΔV/V˙," describes "how much the threshold-crossing time moves after the waveform is perturbed by voltage" — that is threshold-crossing sensitivity, where a large slope makes timing more stable (this is another angle on "fast transitions help"). By contrast, Γ=−sin⁡θ\Gamma=-\sin\thetaΓ=−sinθ describes excess-phase sensitivity: zero at the peak, maximal at the ZC. The two appear contradictory but are actually different questions: threshold crossing asks "when does the edge cross the threshold"; excess phase asks "how much is the limit cycle pushed tangentially." This page, and the design rules across the whole site, follow [P1]'s ISF Γ\GammaΓ (excess phase): ∣Γ∣|\Gamma|∣Γ∣ is maximal at the ZC (the high-slope crossing point) and minimal at the peak. This is exactly what the figure below shows.

Step 2: reading the LC waveform vs. ISF overlay​

The figure below overlays the ideal LC waveform V(θ)=cos⁡θV(\theta)=\cos\thetaV(θ)=cosθ with its ISF Γ(θ)=−sin⁡θ\Gamma(\theta)=-\sin\thetaΓ(θ)=−sinθ:

How to read this figure:

  • Peak/trough (θ=0,π\theta=0,\piθ=0,π, VVV at an extremum, dV/dθ=0dV/d\theta=0dV/dθ=0): Γ=0\Gamma=0Γ=0. Injecting charge here → pure amplitude perturbation → pulled back by amplitude restoration → leaves almost no phase. This is the "safe" phase.
  • Zero crossing, ZC (θ=π/2,3π/2\theta=\pi/2,3\pi/2θ=π/2,3π/2, V=0V=0V=0, slope maximal): ∣Γ∣|\Gamma|∣Γ∣ is maximal. Injecting charge here → pure phase jump → persists permanently. This is the "dangerous" phase.
  • Compare to [P1] Fig. 4: the same impulse applied at the peak versus at the ZC produces completely different tangential/radial decomposition in state space — this is the signature of the oscillator being an LTV (linear time-varying) system (claim C1).

This is a pedagogical toy model (not transistor-level): an idealized LC in the sinusoidal steady state. A real LC, with tank loss and a nonlinear transconductor, will not give a perfect −sin⁡-\sin−sin ISF, but the qualitative conclusion "peak safe, ZC dangerous" holds. Step 3: why "fast transitions" help ring oscillators​

A ring oscillator's waveform is not sinusoidal but closer to a square wave — spending most of its time "pinned at the rail" and rushing through the ZC only during the switching instant (the transition). Its ISF is therefore concentrated near the transition ([P2] Fig. 6, p.793; Fig. 5 shows the peak/lobe narrowing with NNN):

  • On the rail (flat top): V˙≈0\dot V\approx0V˙≈0 should in theory be very sensitive — but the device is usually not conducting / not injecting noise at that moment (α≈0\alpha\approx0α≈0, see device_noise_mapping), so the effective ISF stays small.
  • During the transition: V˙\dot VV˙ is maximal → the bare Γ\GammaΓ is small; but the device is switching at full tilt, and noise is maximal (α\alphaα large).
  • Multiplying the two together: the ring's effective ISF Γeff=Γ⋅α\Gamma_{eff}=\Gamma\cdot\alphaΓeff​=Γ⋅α has its energy concentrated at the transition.

Why a fast transition (steep edge) helps, for two mutually reinforcing reasons:

  1. Shortens the danger window: the faster the transition, the narrower the time window in which the device is "fully on, crossing the edge," so less noise is collected into phase during this "exposure time" → Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​ drops (the ring's Γrms∝N−3/2\Gamma_{rms}\propto N^{-3/2}Γrms​∝N−3/2 trend is also tied to each stage's transition getting steeper; see lc_vs_ring).
  2. Improves threshold-crossing immunity: the steeper the edge, the smaller Δt=ΔV/V˙\Delta t=\Delta V/\dot VΔt=ΔV/V˙ — the same voltage noise produces less timing jitter. This is the same principle behind "fast slew rate → low jitter" on a SerDes data path (see serdes_clocking_connection).

Numerical example (building intuition)​

Using canonical numbers to estimate "the phase difference from the same Δq\Delta qΔq at peak vs. ZC."

Take qmax=1q_{max}=1qmax​=1 pC, Δq=1\Delta q=1Δq=1 fC, f0=5f_0=5f0​=5 GHz. Ideal LC Γ(θ)=−sin⁡θ\Gamma(\theta)=-\sin\thetaΓ(θ)=−sinθ:

  • At the ZC (θ=π/2\theta=\pi/2θ=π/2, Γ=−1\Gamma=-1Γ=−1, maximum sensitivity):
Δϕ=∣Γ∣ Δqqmax=1×10−1510−12=10−3 rad  ⇒  Δt=10−32π⋅5×109=31.8 fs.\Delta\phi=\frac{|\Gamma|\,\Delta q}{q_{max}}=\frac{1\times10^{-15}}{10^{-12}}=10^{-3}\ \text{rad}\;\Rightarrow\;\Delta t=\frac{10^{-3}}{2\pi\cdot5\times10^9}=31.8\ \text{fs}.Δϕ=qmax​∣Γ∣Δq​=10−121×10−15​=10−3 rad⇒Δt=2π⋅5×10910−3​=31.8 fs.
  • At the peak (θ=0\theta=0θ=0, Γ=0\Gamma=0Γ=0): Δϕ=0\Delta\phi=0Δϕ=0 rad, Δt=0\Delta t=0Δt=0 (ideal; real devices show residual leakage).
  • At an intermediate phase (θ=π/6\theta=\pi/6θ=π/6, Γ=−0.5\Gamma=-0.5Γ=−0.5, i.e., canonical example A's Γ=0.5\Gamma=0.5Γ=0.5):
Δϕ=0.5×10−1510−12=5×10−4 rad  ⇒  Δt=15.9 fs.\Delta\phi=\frac{0.5\times10^{-15}}{10^{-12}}=5\times10^{-4}\ \text{rad}\;\Rightarrow\;\Delta t=15.9\ \text{fs}.Δϕ=10−120.5×10−15​=5×10−4 rad⇒Δt=15.9 fs.
  • Intuition: the same 1 fC produces ~32 fs at the ZC but ~0 fs at the peak. Position (phase) matters more than magnitude — this is why, during layout, you should avoid coupling large noise sources (e.g., a switching tail current source, supply ripple) into "low-slope" phase windows.

Worked examples​

The following two problems work out "the same Δq\Delta qΔq, injected at high vs. low slope, how different is Δϕ\Delta\phiΔϕ" with concrete Γ\GammaΓ values, continuing with the canonical qmax=1q_{max}=1qmax​=1 pC, Δq=1\Delta q=1Δq=1 fC, f0=5f_0=5f0​=5 GHz, ideal LC Γ(θ)=−sin⁡θ\Gamma(\theta)=-\sin\thetaΓ(θ)=−sinθ.

Example 1 (high-slope vs. low-slope, ratio of Δϕ\Delta\phiΔϕ for the same Δq\Delta qΔq) Inject the same lump Δq=1\Delta q=1Δq=1 fC at the ZC (θ=π/2\theta=\pi/2θ=π/2, slope maximal, ∣Γ∣=1|\Gamma|=1∣Γ∣=1) and "near the peak" (θ=π/6=30∘\theta=\pi/6=30^\circθ=π/6=30∘, slope small, ∣Γ∣=sin⁡(π/6)=0.5|\Gamma|=\sin(\pi/6)=0.5∣Γ∣=sin(π/6)=0.5). Find Δϕ\Delta\phiΔϕ at each point and their ratio.

Key takeaways​

  • ISF magnitude is, roughly, inversely proportional to waveform slope: small slope (peak) → large ∣Γ∣|\Gamma|∣Γ∣, dangerous; large slope (ZC) → small ∣Γ∣|\Gamma|∣Γ∣, safe.
  • Ideal LC: Γ=−sin⁡θ\Gamma=-\sin\thetaΓ=−sinθ, Γ=0\Gamma=0Γ=0 at the peak, ∣Γ∣|\Gamma|∣Γ∣ maximal at the ZC (see figure).
  • Fast transitions give a double benefit: shortening the "danger window" lowers Γrms\Gamma_{rms}Γrms​, and improving threshold-crossing immunity lowers timing jitter.
  • Same 1 fC lump: ~32 fs when injected at the ZC, ~0 fs at the peak (5 GHz, qmax=1q_{max}=1qmax​=1 pC) — position matters more than magnitude.
  • A ring's effective ISF concentrates at the transition; keep large noise sources away from phases where ∣Γeff∣|\Gamma_{eff}|∣Γeff​∣ is large.
