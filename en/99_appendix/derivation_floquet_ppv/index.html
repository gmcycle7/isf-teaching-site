Floquet / adjoint / PPV: the rigorous foundation of the ISF

Prerequisites / See also: isf_definition (the intuitive definition of the ISF, "projection onto the tangential direction"), phase_vs_amplitude_noise (geometric picture of phase/amplitude perturbations), lti_vs_ltv (why an oscillator is LTV) | Next: ltv_htm (the HTM face of the same ISF), derivation_leeson (comparison with the empirical model)

[P1] introduces the ISF (Impulse Sensitivity Function) via "physical intuition + impulse simulation": poke the oscillator, see how much the phase permanently shifts, and package the result into a periodic function Γ(ω0τ)\Gamma(\omega_0\tau)Γ(ω0​τ). This route is very approachable and is the main line of this site (see isf_definition). But it leaves one thing unresolved: what exactly, mathematically, is "projection onto the tangential (phase) direction"? Why does that particular direction correspond to "zero restoring force, permanent accumulation"? This page fills in that rigorous mathematical foundation.

Honesty note (read this first): The Floquet theory, monodromy matrix, adjoint method, and PPV (Perturbation Projection Vector) on this page are entirely external literature, not among the five PDFs downloaded for this site. The primary sources are [E2] A. Demir, A. Mehrotra, and J. Roychowdhury, "Phase Noise in Oscillators: A Unifying Theory and Numerical Methods for Characterization," IEEE Trans. Circuits Syst. I, vol. 47, no. 5, pp. 655–674, May 2000, and [E3] F. X. Kärtner, "Analysis of White and f−αf^{-\alpha}f−α Noise in Oscillators," Int. J. Circuit Theory Appl., vol. 18, pp. 485–519, 1990. Volume/issue/page/DOI have been verified online ([E2] DOI 10.1109/81.847872, [E3] DOI 10.1002/cta.4490180505). The notation used on this page (v1,B,ξv_1,B,\xiv1​,B,ξ, Floquet-exponent conventions) follows the original sources and is presented here as background framework. This page only supplies the mathematical intuition and skeleton for "why the ISF is a rigorous object" — it does not replace the originals.

This page answers three questions:

  1. What linear equation governs a perturbation near an oscillator's limit cycle?
  2. What is the structure of that equation's solutions (Floquet)? Why must there be a direction with "zero exponent, permanent, undamped"?
  3. Projecting an arbitrary perturbation onto that direction, how do we get ϕ˙=v1T(t)B(t)ξ(t)\dot\phi=v_1^T(t)B(t)\xi(t)ϕ˙​=v1T​(t)B(t)ξ(t), and how does that map back onto the ISF's Γ/qmax\Gamma/q_{max}Γ/qmax​?

Physical intuition (the punchline first): An autonomous oscillator (one with no external clock, one that determines its own phase) has an intrinsic symmetry — time-translation invariance. Shift the entire solution slightly forward or backward in time, and it's still a valid solution. This direction of "sliding a bit along the trajectory" is the phase direction; since nothing pulls it back to some "correct" instant, a perturbation along this direction stays forever. Floquet theory encodes this as "one Floquet exponent is exactly 0," and the PPV v1(t)v_1(t)v1​(t) is the weighting vector that converts "an arbitrary kick" into "how far it moved along that direction." The ISF is nothing more than the scalarized version of the PPV for the specific kick "charge injected into a particular node capacitance."

Step 0: writing the oscillator as a state equation​

Any oscillator (LC, ring, Colpitts...) can be written as a set of first-order ODEs (state-space form):

x˙(t)=f(x(t)),x∈RN.\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=\mathbf{f}\big(\mathbf{x}(t)\big),\qquad \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.x˙(t)=f(x(t)),x∈RN.
  • x\mathbf{x}x is the state vector (e.g., (vC, iL)(v_C,\,i_L)(vC​,iL​): capacitor voltage, inductor current), NNN is the state dimension.
  • f\mathbf{f}f is the circuit's nonlinear vector field (device characteristics + KCL/KVL).
  • Autonomous: f\mathbf{f}f has no explicit ttt-dependence — this is precisely the mathematical signature of "the oscillator determines its own frequency and phase."

At steady state there exists a periodic solution xs(t)=xs(t+T)\mathbf{x}_s(t)=\mathbf{x}_s(t+T)xs​(t)=xs​(t+T), T=1/f0T=1/f_0T=1/f0​, tracing a limit cycle in state space.

  • Dimension check: [x˙]=[x]/s[\dot{\mathbf{x}}]=[\mathbf{x}]/\text{s}[x˙]=[x]/s, [f]=[x]/s[\mathbf{f}]=[\mathbf{x}]/\text{s}[f]=[x]/s ✓ (both sides are "rate of change of state").

Injecting noise/perturbation adds a term:

x˙(t)=f(x(t))+B(t) ξ(t).\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=\mathbf{f}\big(\mathbf{x}(t)\big)+B(t)\,\boldsymbol{\xi}(t).x˙(t)=f(x(t))+B(t)ξ(t).
  • ξ(t)\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)ξ(t) is the vector of perturbation sources (e.g., various noise currents ini_nin​).
  • B(t)B(t)B(t) is the injection/coupling matrix: it specifies which states the perturbation hits, and how strongly. For "current injected into a node capacitance," the corresponding row of BBB is roughly 1/Cnode1/C_{node}1/Cnode​ (converting current into v˙\dot vv˙).
  • Dimension check: [Bξ][B\boldsymbol\xi][Bξ] must be [x]/s[\mathbf{x}]/\text{s}[x]/s. If ξ\xiξ is a current (A) and the corresponding state is a capacitor voltage (V), that row scales as ∼1/C\sim 1/C∼1/C ([A]/[F]=[A]⋅[V/C]=[V/s][\text{A}]/[\text{F}]=[\text{A}]\cdot[\text{V/C}]=[\text{V/s}][A]/[F]=[A]⋅[V/C]=[V/s]) ✓.

Mapping to [P1]'s language: Step 0's B(t)ξ(t)B(t)\boldsymbol\xi(t)B(t)ξ(t) is exactly the step "noise current through a node capacitance becomes v˙\dot vv˙" ([P1] Eq.(9), p.181, the differential form of ΔV=Δq/Cnode\Delta V=\Delta q/C_{node}ΔV=Δq/Cnode​).

Step 1: linearizing near the limit cycle → a linear system with periodic coefficients​

Under a small perturbation, write x(t)=xs(t)+Δx(t)\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}_s(t)+\Delta\mathbf{x}(t)x(t)=xs​(t)+Δx(t) and Taylor-expand f\mathbf{f}f to first order:

x˙s+Δx˙=f(xs)+∂f∂x∣xs(t)⏟≡ A(t)Δx+B(t)ξ(t)+O(Δx2).\dot{\mathbf{x}}_s+\dot{\Delta\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_s)+\underbrace{\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\bigg|_{\mathbf{x}_s(t)}}_{\equiv\,A(t)}\Delta\mathbf{x}+B(t)\boldsymbol\xi(t)+O(\Delta\mathbf{x}^2).x˙s​+Δx˙=f(xs​)+≡A(t)∂x∂f​​xs​(t)​​​Δx+B(t)ξ(t)+O(Δx2).

Since x˙s=f(xs)\dot{\mathbf{x}}_s=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_s)x˙s​=f(xs​) (the steady state itself satisfies the unperturbed equation), the two sides cancel, leaving the linear equation for the perturbation:

Δx˙(t)=A(t) Δx(t)+B(t) ξ(t),A(t)≡∂f∂x∣xs(t).\dot{\Delta\mathbf{x}}(t)=A(t)\,\Delta\mathbf{x}(t)+B(t)\,\boldsymbol\xi(t),\qquad A(t)\equiv\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\bigg|_{\mathbf{x}_s(t)}.Δx˙(t)=A(t)Δx(t)+B(t)ξ(t),A(t)≡∂x∂f​​xs​(t)​.
  • Key observation: A(t)=A(t+T)A(t)=A(t+T)A(t)=A(t+T) is a matrix with periodic coefficients (because it's evaluated on the periodic trajectory xs(t)\mathbf{x}_s(t)xs​(t)). This is exactly why oscillator perturbations are LTV (linear time-varying) rather than LTI — the "system matrix" varies periodically in time, matching the LTV nature that [P1] repeatedly emphasizes (see lti_vs_ltv).
  • Math used: Jacobian linearization; dropping O(Δx2)O(\Delta\mathbf{x}^2)O(Δx2) is the "small perturbation/small noise" assumption, consistent with [P1]'s small-signal assumption.
  • Dimension check: AAA has units 1/s1/\text{s}1/s (∂x˙/∂x\partial\dot{\mathbf x}/\partial\mathbf x∂x˙/∂x), and A ΔxA\,\Delta\mathbf xAΔx is [x]/s[\mathbf x]/\text{s}[x]/s ✓.

The homogeneous part (turning off ξ\boldsymbol\xiξ) is

Δx˙=A(t) Δx,A(t+T)=A(t).\dot{\Delta\mathbf{x}}=A(t)\,\Delta\mathbf{x},\qquad A(t+T)=A(t).Δx˙=A(t)Δx,A(t+T)=A(t).

This is precisely the object studied by Floquet theory: a linear ODE with periodic coefficients.

Step 2: Floquet theory — solution structure and the monodromy matrix​

For the linear system Δx˙=A(t)Δx\dot{\Delta\mathbf x}=A(t)\Delta\mathbf xΔx˙=A(t)Δx, define the state transition matrix Φ(t,t0)\Phi(t,t_0)Φ(t,t0​): it maps a perturbation at time t0t_0t0​ to time ttt, Δx(t)=Φ(t,t0)Δx(t0)\Delta\mathbf x(t)=\Phi(t,t_0)\Delta\mathbf x(t_0)Δx(t)=Φ(t,t0​)Δx(t0​), with Φ(t0,t0)=I\Phi(t_0,t_0)=IΦ(t0​,t0​)=I.

Advancing it over exactly one period gives the monodromy matrix:

M≡Φ(t0+T, t0).M\equiv\Phi(t_0+T,\,t_0).M≡Φ(t0​+T,t0​).
  • Physical meaning: MMM answers "what does a perturbation become after one full cycle?" Its eigenvalues μi\mu_iμi​ are called Floquet multipliers, describing the amplification/attenuation factor per cycle for each perturbation direction.
  • Dimension check: Φ\PhiΦ and MMM are both dimensionless linear maps (state to state) ✓.

Floquet's theorem states that homogeneous solutions can be written as

Δx(t)=∑i=1Nci ui(t) eλit,ui(t)=ui(t+T),\Delta\mathbf{x}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N} c_i\,\mathbf{u}_i(t)\,e^{\lambda_i t},\qquad \mathbf{u}_i(t)=\mathbf{u}_i(t+T),Δx(t)=i=1∑N​ci​ui​(t)eλi​t,ui​(t)=ui​(t+T),

where ui(t)\mathbf{u}_i(t)ui​(t) is the periodic Floquet eigenvector and λi\lambda_iλi​ are the Floquet exponents, related to the multipliers by

μi=eλiT⟺λi=1Tln⁡μi.\mu_i=e^{\lambda_i T}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lambda_i=\frac{1}{T}\ln\mu_i.μi​=eλi​T⟺λi​=T1​lnμi​.
  • How to read λi\lambda_iλi​: Re λi<0\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_i<0Reλi​<0 → the perturbation along that direction decays (stable, e.g., the amplitude direction); Re λi=0\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_i=0Reλi​=0 → neutral, undamped (the phase direction); Re λi>0\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_i>0Reλi​>0 → diverges (should not occur for a stable limit cycle).
  • Dimension check: [λi]=1/s[\lambda_i]=1/\text{s}[λi​]=1/s, [λiT][\lambda_i T][λi​T] dimensionless (the exponent must be dimensionless) ✓.

Step 3: why there must be a λ=0\lambda=0λ=0 direction (the phase direction)​

This is the pivot of the whole theory, and it can be proven by hand. Differentiate the steady-state solution with respect to time: differentiate both sides of x˙s=f(xs)\dot{\mathbf x}_s=\mathbf f(\mathbf x_s)x˙s​=f(xs​) once more with respect to ttt (chain rule):

ddtx˙s=∂f∂x∣xsx˙s⟹ddt(x˙s)=A(t) x˙s.\frac{d}{dt}\dot{\mathbf x}_s=\frac{\partial\mathbf f}{\partial\mathbf x}\bigg|_{\mathbf x_s}\dot{\mathbf x}_s \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dt}\big(\dot{\mathbf x}_s\big)=A(t)\,\dot{\mathbf x}_s.dtd​x˙s​=∂x∂f​​xs​​x˙s​⟹dtd​(x˙s​)=A(t)x˙s​.

In other words, x˙s(t)\dot{\mathbf x}_s(t)x˙s​(t) is itself a solution of the homogeneous perturbation equation Δx˙=A(t)Δx\dot{\Delta\mathbf x}=A(t)\Delta\mathbf xΔx˙=A(t)Δx! And x˙s(t)=x˙s(t+T)\dot{\mathbf x}_s(t)=\dot{\mathbf x}_s(t+T)x˙s​(t)=x˙s​(t+T) is periodic — it carries no exponential growth/decay factor at all, i.e., λ=0\lambda=0λ=0 in eλte^{\lambda t}eλt.

  • Conclusion: the Floquet exponent corresponding to the tangent vector x˙s(t)\dot{\mathbf x}_s(t)x˙s​(t) of the limit cycle is exactly λ1=0\lambda_1=0λ1​=0. Denote it the first principal vector:
u1(t)∝x˙s(t),λ1=0.\mathbf{u}_1(t)\propto\dot{\mathbf x}_s(t),\qquad \lambda_1=0.u1​(t)∝x˙s​(t),λ1​=0.

How to read this figure: the tangential arrow along the limit cycle in the figure is exactly the u1(t)∝x˙s(t)\mathbf u_1(t)\propto\dot{\mathbf x}_s(t)u1​(t)∝x˙s​(t) just proven in this step — it corresponds to λ1=0\lambda_1=0λ1​=0, so a perturbation along this direction neither grows nor decays, and accumulates permanently as phase; the radial arrow (amplitude direction) corresponds to Re λi≥2<0\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{i\ge2}<0Reλi≥2​<0, where the perturbation is pulled back to the limit cycle by a restoring force. This is exactly the rigorous Floquet version of the geometric picture in phase_vs_amplitude_noise (same figure limit_cycle_phase_amplitude.png, lab_01 fig_limit_cycle, pedagogical toy model, 2-D state illustration).

  • Physical meaning: x˙s\dot{\mathbf x}_sx˙s​ is the direction of "moving along the trajectory." Moving along this direction = shifting in time = changing phase. λ1=0\lambda_1=0λ1​=0 mathematically guarantees that "phase perturbation neither amplifies nor decays, and is retained forever" — this is exactly the rigorous basis for [P1]'s expression of "a phase step never disappears" via the unit step u(t−τ)u(t-\tau)u(t−τ) ([P1] Eq.(10), p.182), and for the statement in Step 2 of isf_definition that "the tangential direction = neutral direction, no restoring force."
  • Other directions: the remaining λ2,…\lambda_2,\dotsλ2​,… (amplitude and faster-decaying modes) have Re λi<0\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_i<0Reλi​<0, so the perturbation is pulled back to the limit cycle — this is the rigorous version of "amplitude perturbation decays" ([P4] Fig. 5, p.2126, the amplitude decay function, see phase_vs_amplitude_noise).

This step is the entire secret behind the ISF's "projection onto the tangential direction": this site's isf_definition dots the perturbation with ∂z/∂θ\partial\mathbf z/\partial\theta∂z/∂θ by hand; and ∂z/∂θ∝x˙s\partial\mathbf z/\partial\theta\propto\dot{\mathbf x}_s∂z/∂θ∝x˙s​ is precisely the Floquet principal vector with λ1=0\lambda_1=0λ1​=0. Intuition and rigor line up perfectly here.

Step 4: the adjoint system and the "left eigenvector" v1(t)v_1(t)v1​(t)​

Here's the issue: u1(t)\mathbf{u}_1(t)u1​(t) tells us what the phase direction looks like, but to project an arbitrary kick B(t)ξ(t)B(t)\boldsymbol\xi(t)B(t)ξ(t) onto the phase direction, we need its dual — a left vector capable of "extracting the phase component." This is where the adjoint system comes in.

Define the adjoint equation of the original system:

p˙(t)=−AT(t) p(t).\dot{\mathbf{p}}(t)=-A^{T}(t)\,\mathbf{p}(t).p˙​(t)=−AT(t)p(t).
  • Why −AT-A^T−AT: consider the inner product ⟨p,Δx⟩=pTΔx\langle\mathbf p,\Delta\mathbf x\rangle=\mathbf p^T\Delta\mathbf x⟨p,Δx⟩=pTΔx, and apply the product rule:
ddt(pTΔx)=p˙TΔx+pTΔx˙=(−ATp)TΔx+pT(A Δx)=−pTA Δx+pTA Δx=0.\frac{d}{dt}\big(\mathbf p^T\Delta\mathbf x\big)=\dot{\mathbf p}^T\Delta\mathbf x+\mathbf p^T\dot{\Delta\mathbf x} =(-A^T\mathbf p)^T\Delta\mathbf x+\mathbf p^T(A\,\Delta\mathbf x) =-\mathbf p^TA\,\Delta\mathbf x+\mathbf p^TA\,\Delta\mathbf x=0.dtd​(pTΔx)=p˙​TΔx+pTΔx˙=(−ATp)TΔx+pT(AΔx)=−pTAΔx+pTAΔx=0.

So choosing p˙=−ATp\dot{\mathbf p}=-A^T\mathbf pp˙​=−ATp makes the inner product pTΔx\mathbf p^T\Delta\mathbf xpTΔx conserved — exactly the property we need: "the projection is not corrupted by the system's evolution."

  • Dimension check: [−ATp]=[p]/s[-A^T\mathbf p]=[\mathbf p]/\text{s}[−ATp]=[p]/s ✓ (consistent with p˙\dot{\mathbf p}p˙​).

The adjoint system also has Floquet structure, and the periodic solution corresponding to λ=0\lambda=0λ=0 is denoted v1(t)v_1(t)v1​(t) — it is the dual (left Floquet vector) of the original system's u1(t)\mathbf u_1(t)u1​(t). v1(t)v_1(t)v1​(t) is the PPV (Perturbation Projection Vector). The standard normalization is "the PPV is aligned with the phase direction with unit scale":

v1T(t) x˙s(t)=1(for all t; normalization convention).v_1^{T}(t)\,\dot{\mathbf x}_s(t)=1\qquad\text{(for all }t\text{; normalization convention)}.v1T​(t)x˙s​(t)=1(for all t; normalization convention).
  • Intuition: u1∝x˙s\mathbf u_1\propto\dot{\mathbf x}_su1​∝x˙s​ is "the arrow of the phase direction"; v1v_1v1​ is "the ruler that measures the component along that arrow." The two are calibrated by the equation above so that "moving one unit of time along the trajectory = phase advances by one unit."
  • Practical value of the adjoint: solving the adjoint system's periodic solution once gives you the entire v1(t)v_1(t)v1​(t) in one shot (i.e., the entire ISF); no need to brute-force "fire an impulse at one phase at a time" as in lab_04. The PSS + Pnoise engines inside commercial RF simulators internally use exactly this kind of adjoint/Floquet flow.
  • Note (background): the normalization convention for v1(t)v_1(t)v1​(t) (v1Tx˙s=1v_1^T\dot{\mathbf x}_s=1v1T​x˙s​=1) and the λ\lambdaλ notation follow [E2] Demir 2000 (pp.655–674, DOI 10.1109/81.847872); the citation has been verified, but the internal notation belongs to an external framework.

Step 5: projection → proving ϕ˙=v1T(t)B(t)ξ(t)\dot\phi=v_1^T(t)B(t)\boldsymbol\xi(t)ϕ˙​=v1T​(t)B(t)ξ(t)​

Now apply the "ruler" from Step 4 to the perturbed equation. Demir et al. prove that, near the limit cycle, the state can be written as "a phase shift along the trajectory + a decaying orbital deviation":

x(t)=xs(t+α(t))+y(t),\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}_s\big(t+\alpha(t)\big)+\mathbf{y}(t),x(t)=xs​(t+α(t))+y(t),

where α(t)\alpha(t)α(t) is the phase shift in the time domain (units s), and y(t)\mathbf y(t)y(t) is the decaying orbital-deviation component (lying in the subspace with Re λ<0\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda<0Reλ<0). Substitute this back into x˙=f(x)+Bξ\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf f(\mathbf x)+B\boldsymbol\xix˙=f(x)+Bξ, and use v1v_1v1​ to project out the decaying y\mathbf yy (because v1Tv_1^Tv1T​ is "orthogonal" to those directions — this is the biorthogonality of the left/right Floquet vectors). Only the phase component survives, giving the first-order phase equation:

α˙(t)=v1T(t+α(t)) B(t) ξ(t)  ≈  v1T(t) B(t) ξ(t).\dot{\alpha}(t)=v_1^{T}\big(t+\alpha(t)\big)\,B(t)\,\boldsymbol\xi(t)\;\approx\;v_1^{T}(t)\,B(t)\,\boldsymbol\xi(t).α˙(t)=v1T​(t+α(t))B(t)ξ(t)≈v1T​(t)B(t)ξ(t).

Converting the time-domain phase α\alphaα into the radian phase ϕ=ω0α\phi=\omega_0\alphaϕ=ω0​α (or directly defining ϕ\phiϕ as the dimensionless phase) gives this page's headline result:

 ϕ˙(t)=v1T(t) B(t) ξ(t) \boxed{\ \dot{\phi}(t)=v_1^{T}(t)\,B(t)\,\boldsymbol\xi(t)\ } ϕ˙​(t)=v1T​(t)B(t)ξ(t) ​
  • Term-by-term meaning: ξ(t)\boldsymbol\xi(t)ξ(t) is the raw perturbation (noise current); B(t)B(t)B(t) injects it into state space (via the node capacitance); v1T(t)v_1^T(t)v1T​(t) projects the result onto the phase direction, extracting "how much phase-change rate this particular kick contributes."
  • Why ϕ˙\dot\phiϕ˙​ (rate) rather than ϕ\phiϕ: because the phase is the neutral direction (λ1=0\lambda_1=0λ1​=0), every instantaneous kick is accumulated into the phase "verbatim" — so the perturbation directly gives ϕ˙\dot\phiϕ˙​; to get ϕ\phiϕ you must integrate over time. This is exactly the same as the integral form of [P1] Eq.(11) (convolution_derivation):
ϕ(t)=∫−∞tv1T(τ) B(τ) ξ(τ) dτ.\phi(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}v_1^{T}(\tau)\,B(\tau)\,\boldsymbol\xi(\tau)\,d\tau.ϕ(t)=∫−∞t​v1T​(τ)B(τ)ξ(τ)dτ.
  • Dimension check: ϕ˙\dot\phiϕ˙​ is in rad/s. On the right, v1TBξv_1^TB\boldsymbol\xiv1T​Bξ: taking noise as a current (A), the corresponding row of BBB scales as ∼1/C\sim1/C∼1/C (V/C via A becomes V/s... details depend on normalization), and the overall combination is tuned to rad/s. The exact dimensional bookkeeping depends on the normalization of v1v_1v1​ (whether it absorbs 1/qmax1/q_{max}1/qmax​) (following [E2]'s convention) — see the next step for the mapping.

Step 6: mapping back to the ISF — Γ/qmax\Gamma/q_{max}Γ/qmax​ is the scalarized PPV​

Narrow Step 5 down to [P1]'s specific scenario: a single noise current in(t)i_n(t)in​(t) injected into a single node capacitance CnodeC_{node}Cnode​. In that case:

  • ξ(t)→in(t)\boldsymbol\xi(t)\to i_n(t)ξ(t)→in​(t) (scalar).
  • B(t)→bB(t)\to \mathbf bB(t)→b (a constant vector, nonzero only in the entry corresponding to "the capacitor state at the injection node," with value ∼1/Cnode\sim 1/C_{node}∼1/Cnode​, converting current into v˙\dot vv˙).
  • v1T(t) bv_1^T(t)\,\mathbf bv1T​(t)b is a scalar periodic function — it is exactly Γ(ω0t)/qmax\Gamma(\omega_0 t)/q_{max}Γ(ω0​t)/qmax​.

Therefore

ϕ˙(t)=[v1T(t) b] in(t)  ≡  Γ(ω0t)qmax in(t)⟹ϕ(t)=1qmax∫−∞tΓ(ω0τ) in(τ) dτ.\dot\phi(t)=\big[v_1^{T}(t)\,\mathbf b\big]\,i_n(t)\;\equiv\;\frac{\Gamma(\omega_0 t)}{q_{max}}\,i_n(t) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \phi(t)=\frac{1}{q_{max}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\Gamma(\omega_0\tau)\,i_n(\tau)\,d\tau.ϕ˙​(t)=[v1T​(t)b]in​(t)≡qmax​Γ(ω0​t)​in​(t)⟹ϕ(t)=qmax​1​∫−∞t​Γ(ω0​τ)in​(τ)dτ.

The right-hand form is exactly [P1] Eq.(11), p.182. Comparing the two gives the rigorous correspondence:
